When a government organises a state funeral, it is not merely disposing of a body. It is making a public argument about what the nation is, what it values, and who belongs to it — and in pluralist societies, that argument is rarely settled in advance.

## The ceremony as civic statement

A state funeral is one of the few moments when a government performs its identity in front of the entire population. Flags, music, silence, the order of speakers, the choice of venue — each element is a claim about collective meaning. In countries with an established church or a dominant religious tradition, the default is often a religious ceremony, sometimes without much deliberate thought. Westminster Abbey hosts British state funerals. Notre-Dame has hosted French ones. The National Cathedral in Washington hosts American presidential memorial services despite the constitutional separation of church and state.

The problem is not that these buildings are beautiful or historically resonant. The problem is that the implicit message of holding a national ceremony inside a specific religious structure is that this religion is, at minimum, the ceremonial default of the nation. For citizens who do not share that tradition — secular citizens, members of minority faiths, and nonreligious people who may constitute a substantial fraction of the population — the ceremony speaks *about* them without speaking *to* them. The state, in its most solemn register, is using a language they do not share.

## What secularism actually demands here

Secularism, understood as a procedural commitment to institutional neutrality rather than a metaphysical claim about religion's truth, does not require that state funerals be stripped of all spiritual texture. It requires that the state not privilege one tradition's idiom as the default idiom of public grief. Those are different demands, and conflating them leads to the mistaken view that secular funerals must be cold or empty.

The French Republic offers a useful test case. France's *laïcité* is among the most rigorous secularist frameworks in democratic governance, yet French state funerals have ranged from the heavily religious (de Gaulle's Mass at Notre-Dame) to the deliberately secular (Mitterrand's ceremony at the Panthéon, which included no religious rites). The Panthéon itself is instructive: a building originally consecrated as a church, then reconsecrated as a secular mausoleum of republican heroes, then briefly returned to the church, and finally permanently reclaimed as a secular space. Its history encodes the contested negotiation between republican identity and Catholic inheritance that France has never fully resolved.

## The problem of inherited ritual grammar

Even when a state tries to construct a secular ceremony, it tends to reach for gestures whose origins are religious: the moment of silence (a secularised form of communal prayer), the eulogy (descended from the funeral sermon), the slow march and the lowered flag (military-religious hybrids). This is not hypocrisy; it is the ordinary way that cultures repurpose inherited forms. But it does mean that **secular ceremony is never built from scratch**. It is always a negotiation with the ritual grammar that already exists.

This creates a genuine tension. A secular state that consciously avoids all religious symbolism risks producing ceremonies that feel bureaucratic or affectless — and affectlessness is itself a statement, usually an unwelcome one during collective grief. A secular state that borrows freely from dominant religious tradition without acknowledgment risks signalling that the tradition is not really borrowed but owned. The honest path is to do what designers and architects sometimes call *explicit quotation*: use inherited forms in ways that make the borrowing visible, so that the ceremony acknowledges its sources without endorsing their metaphysical claims.

Nelson Mandela's 2013 memorial in Johannesburg was instructive in a different way. South Africa has no established church and considerable religious diversity, including large Christian, Muslim, Hindu, and traditional African communities. The ceremony included prayers, hymns, and secular tributes in rough sequence, with no single tradition framing the whole. The result was unwieldy in places, but it had the honesty of genuine pluralism: no one tradition was asked to speak for everyone, and no one tradition was excluded from speaking.

## The unrepresented nonreligious

The group most consistently marginalised in state funeral design is not minority religious communities — who are at least sometimes included as token representatives — but **the nonreligious**. In most countries, surveys now show that somewhere between fifteen and forty percent of the population identifies as having no religion. In some Western European countries the figure is higher. Yet secular or humanist voices are rarely included in state ceremonies even as one element among several, let alone invited to shape the overall frame.

This is partly institutional inertia: the bodies that governments consult when designing state ceremonies tend to be established religious organisations with recognised spokespersons, while the nonreligious community lacks equivalent formal structures in most countries. It is partly cultural: "spiritual but not religious" is socially legible in a way that "nonreligious and comfortable with that" often is not. And it is partly a form of motivated blindness: politicians are reluctant to describe any element of a state funeral as secular because the word still reads to many audiences as meaning atheist, and atheism remains electorally cautious territory.

The result is that state funerals systematically misrepresent the population they are supposed to represent. When a head of state dies, the ceremony is meant to be an act of collective mourning. If a third of the country cannot find themselves in the ceremony, the collective is doing something more complicated than mourning together.

## International comparison and what it reveals

Looking across democracies reveals not a spectrum from secular to religious, but a complicated patchwork of local compromises. India's state funerals follow a largely Hindu ceremonial grammar for Hindu politicians and accommodate the traditions of others, reflecting the country's official secularism in theory but its demographic weight in practice. Israel's state funerals are deeply embedded in Jewish ritual, which poses a specific problem given that Arab citizens constitute about twenty percent of the population. Japan's state funerals have been controversial in recent decades precisely because of the difficulty of distinguishing Shinto ritual, which carries specific constitutional complications, from national ceremony.

The United States presents a particular paradox. The First Amendment is unusually strict about state establishment of religion, and American presidents routinely profess personal faith in a way that would be unusual in many European democracies. State memorial services are held in the National Cathedral — an Episcopal church — with the apparent consensus that because it is sometimes called the national cathedral it counts as a civic rather than sectarian space. This is a polite fiction, and it is maintained because the alternative — acknowledging that the United States has no true ceremonial secular space at national scale — would require either building one or admitting that the secular framework and the lived practice of American civil religion have never quite aligned.

## Designing for genuine pluralism

What would a state funeral look like if designed with genuine secular neutrality as the starting constraint? A few principles emerge from the cases above.

The venue matters. A purpose-built civic hall, a grand public square, or a secular civic building sends a different signal from a cathedral, even if the cathedral is aesthetically superior. France's Panthéon and, in a different register, the Lincoln Memorial in Washington suggest that secular spaces can carry the weight of national grief when the culture has invested in them over time.

The frame matters more than the content. A ceremony that opens and closes with secular civic language — appeals to shared history, democratic values, or national continuity — can include religious readings or prayers as contributions from specific communities without those elements defining the whole. The key is that no single tradition provides the master narrative into which others are inserted as guests.

Silence is underused. A minute of genuine, unscripted collective silence is one of the most inclusive acts a secular state can perform, because it does not prescribe what is thought or felt during that time. It is not a prayer directed to a specific god. It is not an affirmation of any metaphysical claim. It is shared time, which is the nearest thing to a universal ritual that a pluralist society possesses.

The honest acknowledgment that a society contains people who understand death differently — and that the state cannot adjudicate between those understandings — is not a weakness in a ceremony. It is a form of respect. What a secular state owes its citizens at moments of collective grief is not a single coherent metaphysical story but a ceremony that does not tell any of them that their way of understanding the moment is the wrong one.
