When you say that two roses are both red, you seem to be saying something true. But what exactly makes it true? There is a rose over here, a rose over there, and then there is—what? A shared redness hanging between them, or two distinct red-things that merely resemble each other? This question sits at the foundation of metaphysics and touches epistemology, philosophy of science, and the limits of abstraction.

## The problem of properties

Every ordinary description invokes properties. The coffee is hot. The argument is valid. The particle has spin-1/2. Each of these attributions seems to pick out something real about the world—not just a word, but a feature that the object genuinely has. Metaphysicians call these features **properties**, and the central question is whether properties are things in their own right or whether talk of them can be reduced to something less extravagant.

This is not merely terminological housekeeping. If properties exist as full-blown entities, the world is populated not just by particular things—this mug, that electron—but by abstract or quasi-abstract items shared across many particulars. If they do not, we need a replacement account of what grounds the truth of "the two roses are both red," and that replacement faces its own difficulties.

## Realism about universals

The oldest systematic answer is **universalism**, associated with Plato's Forms and refined by Aristotle and later by Bertrand Russell and David Armstrong. On this view, properties are **universals**: entities that can be wholly present in multiple locations at once. When two roses are both red, they each instantiate the universal Redness, and that single universal is literally what they have in common.

Armstrong's version is immanent realism: universals exist only when instantiated—there is no universal Unicornhood floating free of reality—but genuine universals there are, and they explain laws of nature by grounding real relations between universals. "All copper conducts electricity" is not merely a regularity but reflects a necessitation relation between the universal Copper and the universal Electrical Conductance. This gives laws genuine necessity rather than mere coincidence.

The costs of universalism are familiar. It requires that a single entity be wholly present in many places simultaneously, which strains ordinary notions of location. It also demands a primitive instantiation relation to link particulars to universals—and that relation is itself a universal, risking a vicious regress (Bradley's regress). Armstrong's response is that instantiation is not a third entity but a fundamental tie, but critics find this move underdeveloped.

## Trope theory

A competing view rejects the idea that two roses share a single redness. Instead, each rose has its own private redness—a **trope**, or abstract particular. The redness of this rose is numerically distinct from the redness of that rose; they merely resemble each other, perhaps exactly. Tropes are sometimes called "modes," "moments," or "individual accidents" in older vocabulary.

Trope theory has genuine attractions. Tropes are particulars, so they can be located where their bearer is, removing the metaphysical awkwardness of a multiply-located universal. Causation becomes tractable: it is this particular mass, not mass-in-general, that causes this particular acceleration. And scientific practice often looks more tropist than universalist—physicists measure the charge of this electron, not Charge as such.

The challenge trope theory faces is accounting for resemblance. If there are no universals, two exact duplicates must be held together by a primitive resemblance relation—or by tropes of resemblance, which can generate its own regress. Trope nominalists typically treat resemblance as primitive, which is an ontological cost they must openly acknowledge rather than dissolve.

The deeper question is whether tropes are genuinely less mysterious than universals, or whether they merely relocate the mystery. Saying "this redness and that redness are exactly alike" seems to gesture at a shared nature—redness—that the theory was supposed to do without.

## Resemblance nominalism and class nominalism

If you want to reject both universals and tropes and hold that only ordinary particulars exist, you need some other account of what makes two roses red. **Class nominalism** says: to be red just is to be a member of the class of red things. The property red just is the set of all red particulars. This seems elegantly deflationary—sets exist in standard mathematics—but the account is circular. The set of red things is defined by picking out the right members; but which members are the right ones? The ones that are red. We are back where we started.

**Resemblance nominalism** tries to do better. Properties are not classes defined by predication but groups of things that resemble a paradigm case or resemble each other to a sufficient degree. Redness is the group of things that relevantly resemble paradigmatically red objects. This avoids appealing to the property itself in its own definition, and it has a good empiricist pedigree—resemblance seems observable in a way universals are not.

But resemblance nominalism faces the **coextension problem**: two properties might be shared by exactly the same particulars—say, "creature with a heart" and "creature with a kidney" pick out the same animals—yet they seem to be different properties. If properties just are resemblance-classes, coextensive properties collapse into one, which gets the individuation wrong. The universalist can distinguish them cleanly: the universal Heart is different from the universal Kidney. The nominalist must work harder.

## Why the debate matters outside the seminar room

It would be easy to treat this as purely academic jousting. It is not. The metaphysics of properties ramifies into philosophy of science in concrete ways.

Laws of nature, as noted, look different depending on your view. If properties are universals related by necessitation, laws are genuinely necessary and support counterfactuals robustly: if this were copper, it would conduct. If properties are resemblance-classes, laws reduce to regularities and counterfactuals require additional machinery to ground them—typically possible-worlds semantics of the Lewisian kind, which brings its own metaphysical commitments.

Natural kind terms are another pressure point. When chemists say "water is H₂O," they seem to be identifying a property shared by all water samples everywhere and at all times—a universal, if anything is. **Semantic externalism** (Putnam's insight that meaning is partly fixed by the world rather than by the speaker's mental states) pairs naturally with realism about natural kinds and thus with realism about universals or at least about stable, mind-independent properties. A thoroughgoing nominalist has to explain what makes "water" track the same stuff across contexts without appealing to a shared nature.

Causation matters too. The received view in philosophy of science holds that causal explanations cite properties, not just things: the wire melted because of its electrical resistance, not merely because of this wire. If properties are real features of the world, this makes sense. If they are useful fictions or mere predicative habits, then causal talk may be more projective than it appears.

## The sparse versus abundant distinction

One development that cuts across the universalist/nominalist divide is the distinction between **sparse** and **abundant** properties, due largely to David Lewis. Abundant properties correspond to every predicate that makes sense: "being a rock or being a Tuesday" picks out an abundant property, however gerrymandered. Sparse properties are the genuine joints in nature—the properties that figure in laws, ground causation, and explain resemblance. Lewis, despite being a nominalist about universals, embraced sparse properties (in his case, as classes of possibilia) because science seems to require them.

This move acknowledges that not all predicates carve reality equally well, which is something common sense and science both insist on. The predicate "grue" (green if examined before time T, blue otherwise) does not track a real property in the same way "green" does. Explaining that difference—what makes some properties natural—is one of the live problems in the field and connects directly to debates about projectability in induction.

## Functionalism's stake in the question

The metaphysics of properties is not just a concern for philosophers of science or logicians. It shapes debates in philosophy of mind as well. **Functionalism** holds that mental states are defined by their functional roles—their causal relations to inputs, outputs, and each other—rather than by their physical constitution. A pain is whatever state tends to be caused by tissue damage and tends to cause avoidance behaviour, regardless of substrate.

This is a claim about properties. If mental properties are functional properties and functional properties are genuine universals or robust tropes, then mental properties are real in their own right and not reducible to physical properties—even if every mental event is a physical event. This is the standard multiple-realizability argument for the autonomy of psychology. If, on the other hand, properties are merely predicates or resemblance-classes, the argument for autonomy needs reformulating, because there is no universal Painhood waiting to be realised in silicon or carbon alike.

The question of what properties are turns out to be inseparable from questions about reduction, levels of description, and scientific ontology more broadly.

## Where the debate stands

No consensus has emerged, and it is worth being clear about why. The core difficulty is that the competing positions each explain some data cleanly while struggling with other data. Universalism handles laws and causation well but pays a steep price in fundamental ontology. Trope theory handles particularity and causation well but faces the resemblance regress. Nominalism is ontologically lean but struggles with coextension and natural kinds.

Progress has come not from decisive refutations but from increasingly precise identification of what each theory costs. That is how metaphysics typically advances: not by knockdown argument but by making the trade-offs explicit so that theoretical virtues—parsimony, explanatory power, coherence with science—can be properly weighed.

For a publication committed to clear thinking about foundations, the metaphysics of properties is a useful case study in what rigorous philosophical inquiry actually looks like. It is not idle speculation about medieval angels. It is a structured argument about the architecture of the world that underlies every true claim science or ordinary language ever makes.
