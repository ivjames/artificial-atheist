The school uniform sits at an unexpectedly sharp intersection of secular governance and personal identity. Disputes over headscarves, crucifixes, turbans, and kippot in state classrooms have reached supreme courts on three continents, and they keep returning because they expose something the broader secularism debate often leaves implicit: neutrality is easier to declare than to achieve in a room full of twelve-year-olds.

## What dress codes are actually doing

A dress code is never merely aesthetic. It signals whose presence in a school is unremarkable and whose requires negotiation. When France's 2004 law banned "ostentatious" religious signs from state schools, it did not ban all religious expression uniformly in practice. A small cross on a chain was permissible; a hijab was not. The distinction the law drew was between signs that "conspicuously" manifest religious affiliation and those that do not — but conspicuousness is not a neutral category. It is calibrated to what the dominant culture reads as normal.

This is not an argument that France was wrong, but it is an argument that the law was doing more than it said. It was expressing a particular theory of what secular public space looks like and treating the visual markers of minority faiths as categorically different from the quiet presence of majority-faith symbols. Critics, including the UN Human Rights Committee in its 2018 review of individual complaints, noted that the practical burden fell overwhelmingly on Muslim girls, not on students of other faiths.

## The two competing secular frameworks

The debate over school clothing is really a debate between two rival models of secularism. The first, often associated with the French *laïcité* tradition, treats secular public institutions as spaces that should be visually and symbolically free of religious markers. Religion is real, legitimate, and privately held — but public institutions belong to all citizens equally, and equality requires a shared, unmarked appearance. On this view, asking a student to remove a religious garment at the school gate is not an attack on their identity but a fair condition of participation in shared civic life.

The second model, more common in anglophone liberal democracies, treats individual religious expression as a right that the state must accommodate unless it can show a concrete harm. On this view, a hijab or a turban does no harm to other students and may not be regulated simply because the state prefers a uniform appearance. The burden is on the state to justify the restriction, not on the student to justify the practice. Canada's approach in *Multani v. Commission scolaire Marguerite-Bourgeoys* (2006), which protected a Sikh student's right to wear a kirpan under strict conditions, exemplifies this logic.

Neither framework is incoherent. Each reflects genuine values. The laïcité model takes seriously the risk that religious coercion — including within-community pressure on girls to cover — can be imported into institutions that should protect individual autonomy. The accommodation model takes seriously the risk that state-imposed uniformity can itself be coercive, effectively demanding that minority students suppress identity as the price of education. What makes dress-code cases so difficult is that both risks are real.

## The problem of exit and compulsion

Schools are compulsory. This distinguishes them from most other secular public spaces. A secular workplace that bans religious attire gives an employee a hard choice, but employment is in principle voluntary and alternatives exist. A child in a country with compulsory education has no meaningful exit option. The state is not merely setting terms for participation in an optional benefit; it is setting conditions for access to a legal requirement.

This asymmetry matters. When Turkey's Constitutional Court upheld the headscarf ban in state universities in 1989, it was at least addressing adults who chose to attend higher education. When Germany's individual *Länder* debated teacher headscarves in state schools, the question was partly whether the state, as an employer, could require neutrality of its agents — a defensible position with its own logic. But applying restrictive dress codes to children who must attend school places the weight of a contested political settlement on individuals who did not choose to be part of that settlement and cannot leave it.

**Compulsion changes the ethics of the uniform rule.** A state that requires school attendance and then imposes clothing rules that conflict with sincere religious practice is making a coercive demand. Whether that demand is justified depends entirely on what is being achieved by the restriction — and "secular appearance" is a thinner justification than "preventing disruption" or "protecting students from identifiable coercion."

## What evidence exists on harm

Proponents of restrictive dress codes often make empirical claims: that visible religious symbols disrupt classroom cohesion, that they signal group membership in ways that generate conflict, or that they function as tools of social pressure on students who would rather not wear them. These are testable propositions, and the evidence for them is much weaker than advocates typically suggest.

Studies of French schools following the 2004 law found little measurable improvement in integration outcomes and some evidence that the exclusion of students who refused to comply deepened rather than reduced their alienation. Research on multicultural classrooms more broadly suggests that visible difference, managed well by trained teachers, does not inherently produce conflict. The harm narrative frequently runs ahead of the data. That does not settle the question — social dynamics are complex, and absence of clear evidence is not evidence of absence — but it should temper confidence in purely empirical justifications for restrictive policies.

The more serious empirical concern runs the other way. Girls who are excluded from school because they refuse to remove a garment required by their family, or who are withdrawn by parents unwilling to accept state dress rules, lose access to exactly the education that is most likely to expand their autonomy over time. A secular policy justified partly by concern for girls' freedom can, in specific cases, make those girls less free.

## How states have tried to navigate this

The comparative record shows no single winning formula, but it does reveal which distinctions tend to hold up and which tend to collapse.

Restrictions tied to **functional safety** — no loose scarves near machinery, no face coverings where identity verification is legally required — are the most defensible and attract the least controversy. They apply equally across all garments and are not calibrated to religious content.

Restrictions tied to the conduct of **state employees rather than students** are more defensible than blanket student bans. A state can coherently argue that teachers, as its agents, should present a religiously unmarked face, without imposing the same requirement on the children in their care. Germany's *Länder* have varied on this, and the European Court of Human Rights has generally given states a margin of appreciation on employee neutrality rules.

Restrictions aimed at **all students uniformly** — the French model — are the most contested and the least empirically supported as tools of integration. They are most coherent when understood not as pragmatic measures but as expressions of a foundational constitutional identity: this is what French public space looks like, and schools are part of public space. One may accept or reject that constitutional identity, but one should be honest that the argument is philosophical, not empirical.

## What the dispute reveals about secular governance

The school uniform debate is a stress test for any secular framework because it forces the question: when the state claims to be neutral, neutral relative to what baseline? A classroom in which no student wears a headscarf looks neutral to someone for whom headscarves are unexpected, and conspicuous to someone for whom they are ordinary. The baseline is never given by nature; it is chosen. And who chooses it, and in whose image, is a political question that secular theory cannot dissolve by invoking neutrality.

This does not mean that all dress policies are equally arbitrary or that states cannot draw defensible lines. It means that the lines must be justified openly, on grounds that can be examined and challenged, rather than presented as natural consequences of a neutral secular starting point. The student standing at the school gate in a garment that marks her as different is not the problem the secular state is solving. She is a citizen whose full inclusion is the goal secular education is supposed to serve. Whether a particular dress policy advances or undermines that goal is an empirical and ethical question — and it deserves to be treated as one.
