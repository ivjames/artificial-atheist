The public hospital is one of the most intimate spaces the state enters. Patients arrive vulnerable, often unable to shop elsewhere, and dependent on institutions whose values they may not share. When those institutions are religious, a structural tension emerges that neither goodwill nor legal formulas have fully resolved.

## The scale of the problem

Religious bodies own or operate a striking share of hospital infrastructure in otherwise secular democracies. In the United States, Catholic Health Association members account for roughly one in six hospital beds. In Australia, Catholic and other faith-based providers run about a third of publicly funded hospitals. Ireland's public system has only recently begun disentangling itself from church-owned facilities that receive nearly all their funding from the state.

This is not a historical accident. Religious orders built hospitals when states would not, trained nurses when no secular institution existed to do so, and maintained rural services that private capital found unprofitable. The infrastructure was donated or heavily subsidised into existence. Governments later agreed to fund it rather than duplicate it. The result is a large public good sitting inside a private religious shell — and patients who have no practical alternative.

## What religious ownership actually restricts

The restrictions vary by tradition and institution, but the most documented cluster around reproductive and end-of-life care. Catholic facilities operating under the **Ethical and Religious Directives** (ERDs) issued by the US Conference of Catholic Bishops will not perform abortions, will not provide contraceptive sterilisation, and will not participate in assisted dying even where state law permits it. Some will not refer patients to facilities that do. Similar constraints apply in Australian Catholic hospitals regarding voluntary assisted dying laws that have now passed in every Australian state.

These are not marginal cases. A woman presenting after a miscarriage that has not yet completed may be denied a uterine evacuation on the grounds that fetal cardiac activity persists — a protocol that follows the ERDs but diverges from mainstream obstetric guidance. A patient asking about tubal ligation after a caesarean section may be refused without being told why. A terminally ill patient eligible under state law for assisted dying may be informed only that the option does not exist at this facility.

The problem is not that religious institutions hold these positions. They are entitled to. The problem is that a patient who is effectively captive — geographically, financially, or by emergency — has no meaningful ability to go elsewhere.

## The neutrality argument and its limits

One response from secular liberals is to invoke institutional neutrality: the state funds the service, not the doctrine, and employees are free to hold any private belief. On this view, religious hospitals are simply contractors delivering a public good under contract terms the state can tighten.

This has some force. Governments can and do attach conditions to funding — requiring that referrals be made even where treatment is declined, or that patients be informed of all legal options regardless of what the hospital will provide. The Victorian government in Australia made access to voluntary assisted dying a condition of receiving public funds, which forced the hand of some Catholic facilities.

But the neutrality argument underestimates structural power. A contract condition that says "you must refer" does not help a patient who does not know she is entitled to ask, does not speak the language well enough to press the point, or is in active haemorrhage. The state's ability to discipline a hospital after the fact is not the same as the patient's ability to receive care in the moment. **Formal neutrality and material neutrality are different things**, and in healthcare the gap between them is often measured in physical harm.

## The conscience clause and its expansion

A related problem is the individual **conscience clause** — legal protections for clinicians who decline to participate in procedures that conflict with their religious beliefs. These exist in most liberal democracies and are, in principle, defensible. A doctor should not be compelled to perform an abortion against their conscience any more than a soldier should be compelled to kill civilians.

The question is whether conscience protections, when aggregated across a religious institution, can effectively nullify a patient's legal right. If a rural hospital has one obstetrician, and that obstetrician declines to perform terminations, and no ambulance transfer protocol exists, the patient's right exists on paper and nowhere else. Courts in the US and UK have generally required that institutional conscience claims be balanced against patient access, but the balance-point is fiercely contested and varies by jurisdiction.

There is also a scope creep worth noting. Conscience clauses were originally designed for direct participation in a procedure. They have since been argued to cover referral, prescription, dispensing, and in some cases even providing information. The further the protection moves from the act itself toward the information environment around it, the more it begins to resemble **institutional veto power over patient knowledge** rather than protection of individual moral integrity.

## The secular alternative: what it would actually cost

Critics of religious hospital entanglement sometimes imply that the solution is obvious: the state should simply build secular public hospitals. This is less simple than it sounds. In Ireland, the decade-long negotiation over the National Maternity Hospital's planned co-location with a religious landowner illustrated how difficult it is to disentangle governance from real estate, staff culture, and institutional history even when the political will exists. The eventual resolution — a new secular entity with its own board — came only after years of public pressure and required primary legislation.

In rural and regional areas the problem is sharper. A government that closes a religious hospital because it will not provide assisted dying may leave a community with no hospital at all. The secular principle and the practical welfare of patients can point in opposite directions, and honest secularism has to sit with that tension rather than pretend it dissolves on contact with principle.

What this suggests is that the funding-conditions route, for all its imperfections, is likely the main practical lever in the medium term. **Conditions need to be specific, monitored, and enforced** — not aspirational language in a funding agreement that no one audits. Minimum requirements for referral, mandatory patient information standards, and clear protocols for transferring patients who need care the institution will not provide are all achievable without requiring religious bodies to perform procedures they find objectionable. They do not resolve the underlying tension, but they reduce its cost to the most vulnerable.

## What the secularist position actually demands here

Secularism, properly understood, does not require that religious institutions be driven from healthcare. It requires that the state not use public power to privilege religious practice over citizen rights, and that patients retain effective — not merely nominal — access to the full range of legal medical options.

This is a more demanding standard than religious institutions often accept and a less demanding one than some secularist advocates press for. It does not mean that every hospital must provide every legal service. It means that a patient's legal entitlements must not effectively disappear because the publicly funded hospital in their area operates under a religious charter. The gap between those two positions is where most of the real policy work lives.

The hospital is not a church, even when a church owns it. When the state pays the bills and the patient has no alternative, the obligations of pluralism run in both directions — and the direction that most needs watching is the one where the patient is lying in the bed.
