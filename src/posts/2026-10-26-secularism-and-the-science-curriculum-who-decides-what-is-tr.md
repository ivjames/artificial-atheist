The science classroom is one of the few places a democratic state makes a positive claim about what is true. That claim brings secularism into an unusually sharp conflict — not just about whose prayers are said or whose symbols hang on the wall, but about whose account of reality the next generation inherits.

## The conflict is not simply religion versus science

It is tempting to frame disputes over evolution, cosmology, or human origins as a straightforward collision between faith and evidence. That framing obscures more than it reveals. Many religious believers accept evolutionary biology without difficulty; many creationist lobbying campaigns are partly political rather than theological. The real tension is institutional: who holds the authority to determine what a state-funded curriculum teaches as established knowledge?

In the United States, the sequence of cases from *Epperson v. Arkansas* (1968) through *Edwards v. Aguillard* (1987) to *Kitzmiller v. Dover* (2005) shows the courts repeatedly ruling that creationism and its successor, intelligent design, are religious positions dressed in scientific language. The rulings rest on the Establishment Clause, but the underlying logic is epistemological: public science education cannot be governed by the internal doctrines of particular religious communities, because those doctrines are not validated by the methods science uses to validate anything else.

This does not mean secular curricula are infallible or that scientific consensus is beyond revision. It means that the revision process — peer review, replication, independent testing — must remain open and methodologically consistent. A community's theological commitments do not constitute evidence in that process, however sincerely they are held.

## How "teach the controversy" arguments actually work

The most sophisticated challenge to secular science education in recent decades has not been outright denial but the invitation to "teach the controversy." The rhetorical move is to present a manufactured dispute as though it were a live scientific debate, then invoke academic freedom or fairness to demand space for the alternative.

The move is worth examining on its own terms. Genuine scientific controversies — about the pace of evolution, the mechanisms of speciation, the timeline of hominin divergence — absolutely belong in a science classroom. Disagreement within a methodology is how that methodology works. What does not belong is disagreement between a methodology and a set of claims that do not engage with it. Young-earth creationism does not offer a competing scientific hypothesis about geological strata; it asserts that the strata tell a different story because a different kind of authority supersedes physical evidence. That is a coherent position, but it is not a scientific one, and pretending otherwise misleads students about what science is.

**Epistemic laundering** is the useful term here: taking a claim that derives its force from religious authority and processing it through the language of academic balance until it acquires an appearance of scientific legitimacy it has not earned. Secular science education needs a clear principle to resist this, and the principle is not anti-religious. It is simply that the methods used to adjudicate a claim must be appropriate to the kind of claim being made.

## The harder cases: psychology, sex education, and the social sciences

Evolution and cosmology generate the loudest fights, but some of the most practically consequential disputes occur in areas where scientific consensus is newer or methodologically messier. Sex education curricula, the psychology of gender identity, the neuroscience of addiction — these are areas where religious objections sometimes coincide with genuine empirical uncertainty, and where the secular state's epistemological authority is legitimately harder to invoke with confidence.

Consider abstinence-only sex education. Multiple systematic reviews have found it does not reduce rates of teen pregnancy or sexually transmitted infections. The evidence base is solid enough that major medical associations have formally opposed it. Here the secular principle is relatively easy to apply: a curriculum shaped by a theological view of sexuality, contradicted by the available evidence, is being imposed on all students regardless of their families' beliefs.

But consider a case where the science itself is actively contested — say, the long-term psychological outcomes of particular interventions in adolescent mental health. A religious community that disputes a curriculum's claims in such an area may be wrong, or may be raising a legitimate concern about premature consensus. Secularism as an epistemological principle does not automatically resolve this. What it requires is that the dispute be settled by the standards of evidence that apply to contested empirical claims, not by the theological commitments of any party.

## Religious schools and the problem of partial exemption

Most liberal democracies allow religious schools to exist, either privately funded or state-supported with varying levels of curriculum oversight. The question of how much exemption from secular science standards these schools may claim is unresolved and genuinely difficult.

A fully private religious school operating without public funding presents one set of considerations. A state-funded faith school — common in England, Ireland, and elsewhere — presents quite another. When public money funds an institution, the case for requiring that institution to teach the publicly validated account of reality is strong. Students in those schools are future citizens; they will vote, hold jobs, and make decisions that affect others. A science education that systematically misrepresents biological or geological evidence disadvantages them and, if it distorts their reasoning about evidence generally, disadvantages everyone else.

The counterargument is that religious communities have a right to transmit their beliefs to their children, including beliefs that conflict with scientific consensus. This right is real and must be respected. The question is where it ends. Teaching a child that their community holds a particular view of human origins is not the same as teaching them that view is scientifically validated when it is not. Secular education policy can accommodate the former while insisting on the latter. The difficulty is that the distinction requires regulators to make judgments about what counts as scientific validation — judgments that religious lobbying groups will contest as themselves ideologically motivated.

## The global picture: secularism is not a Western default

Debates about science curricula in secular states are not confined to North America and Western Europe. In Turkey, the 2017 removal of evolution from the secondary school curriculum by an Islamist-influenced government illustrated how the question plays out in states where secularism itself is politically contested. In India, textbook revisions under successive governments have at various points introduced or removed content on ancient Hindu cosmology in ways that blur the line between cultural heritage and scientific claim. In Russia, the Orthodox Church has periodically lobbied for reduced emphasis on evolution.

What these cases share is a pattern in which religious or civilisational nationalism uses school curricula as a site for asserting symbolic authority over the state's account of truth. The stakes are partly about science education and partly about something larger: whether the state's epistemic institutions are governed by methods that are, in principle, available to anyone regardless of their background, or whether they reflect the particular narrative of a dominant religious community.

This is where secularism earns its value as a procedural commitment rather than an anti-religious one. A secular science curriculum is not one that insists on atheism; it is one that insists on using the same evidentiary standards for all claims, regardless of their source.

## What secular education actually owes to religious students

The secular position on science curricula is sometimes presented — and sometimes practiced — as though it requires treating religious belief as simply false and leaving students who hold it isolated or embarrassed. This is both bad pedagogy and a misunderstanding of what secular education requires.

Science education can acknowledge, honestly, that evolutionary biology contradicts a literal reading of certain scriptural texts. It can explain why the scientific community finds the evidence for evolution overwhelming. It does not need to tell students that their families' beliefs are contemptible or that the question of meaning and purpose is settled by natural selection. Those are philosophical moves that science education is not equipped to make and should not attempt.

The secular science classroom owes religious students the same thing it owes everyone else: an accurate account of what the evidence shows, explained with enough clarity that they can evaluate it for themselves. What it cannot offer is a false balance — a pretence that the evidence is evenly divided when it is not — without betraying the epistemic function it exists to perform.

**Intellectual honesty** is not the same as intellectual aggression. A teacher who explains that the age of the Earth is 4.5 billion years, and why geologists are confident of this, is not attacking anyone's faith. A teacher who implies that a student's religious upbringing makes them epistemically inferior is doing something different and worse. Good secular education distinguishes between these two things. The former is mandatory; the latter is a failure.

## The underlying principle

Science curricula are, in the end, a test case for a specific and demanding version of secularism: the claim that the state's institutions can produce and transmit knowledge by methods that do not depend on any particular religious authority, and that this capacity is worth defending not because religion is wrong about everything but because it is the wrong kind of institution for settling empirical questions.

That principle is uncomfortable for anyone who thinks truth is ultimately guaranteed by revelation rather than evidence. But it is not hostile to religion as such. It is a condition for a shared epistemic commons — a domain in which citizens with radically different ultimate commitments can nonetheless argue about evidence together. Without it, every scientific question becomes a proxy war between communities, and the curriculum becomes whatever the most powerful faction decides.

Secularism in the science classroom is, ultimately, a claim about method. That claim needs to be made clearly, defended rigorously, and applied consistently — to all communities, not selectively against those currently out of political favour.
