Religious organisations in most liberal democracies pay little or no tax. That fact is so familiar it barely registers as a policy choice. But it is one, with real fiscal and philosophical consequences that secularism cannot ignore.

## What exemptions actually consist of

Tax exemptions for religious bodies are not a single concession but a cluster of distinct privileges. In the United States, for example, recognised religious organisations are exempt from federal income tax under Section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code, but they also enjoy a separate category — Section 501(c)(3) applies to charities generally, while churches get additional protections that ordinary nonprofits do not. They are not required to file annual financial disclosures (Form 990). Ministers can exclude housing allowances from taxable income under the "parsonage exemption." Religious schools often receive property tax relief at the state level. Donors who give to religious bodies receive deductions.

Each of these is a distinct mechanism, and they stack. A megachurch with a multimillion-dollar campus, a media operation, a school, and a bookshop may pay almost no tax on any of it, while a secular nonprofit with identical charitable outputs files detailed public disclosures and competes for the same donor base without the same structural advantages.

## The neutrality fiction

The standard justification for these exemptions is that the state is being *neutral* — neither favouring nor penalising religion. This framing has a surface plausibility. If the state taxed religious organisations but not secular charities, it would face a legitimate complaint about selective treatment. Treating religion like other nonprofits, or better, prevents the government from appearing hostile to faith.

But neutrality is not what the current system achieves. **Non-taxation is not the same as equal treatment.** When a religious body receives an exemption that an otherwise identical secular body does not — as with the disclosure-free status of churches or the minister's housing allowance — the state has not stepped back; it has stepped in on one side. The government is making a specific judgment that religious activity deserves a form of fiscal support that comparable secular activity does not.

The philosopher Martha Nussbaum draws a useful distinction between **formal neutrality** (the same rule applies to everyone) and **substantive neutrality** (the same underlying burden or benefit is experienced by everyone). Current exemption regimes often achieve the former only at the cost of the latter. Exempting "all nonprofits" sounds formally neutral, but if the subcategory of religious nonprofits receives additional protections unavailable to secular ones, substantive neutrality has been abandoned.

## What the treasury figures show

In 2012, the sociologist Ryan Cragun and colleagues published an estimate, widely cited and disputed, that U.S. religious organisations received roughly $71 billion annually in tax subsidies. The methodology has been critiqued — some estimates are lower, some higher depending on which exemptions are included — but the order of magnitude is not seriously contested. Property tax exemptions alone, aggregated across fifty states, represent billions of dollars annually that local governments forgo, shifting the burden to other property owners.

This matters for the secularist argument because it reframes the debate. The question is no longer simply whether the state *persecutes* religion — of course it should not — but whether the state *subsidises* it. Those are different questions. A commitment to separation of church and state that is comfortable with large-scale fiscal subsidy is not really committed to separation; it is committed to the absence of overt discrimination while tolerating active financial entanglement.

The counterargument here is genuine and worth taking seriously. Religious organisations do provide public goods: food banks, addiction recovery programmes, disaster relief, schooling, social services. Exemptions, on this view, are a rough proxy for the social value those services produce. If the state had to provide all the services that religious charities currently deliver, the tax cost might exceed the revenue gained by taxing them. The exemption is, on this reading, a net fiscal win for the public even if it looks like a subsidy.

This argument has empirical weight in some cases and much less in others. A small Quaker meeting that runs a homeless shelter has a plausible case. A large prosperity-gospel church that spends the overwhelming majority of its income on facilities, salaries, and media production — while providing minimal charitable services — does not. The problem is that the current system does not distinguish between them, and the special disclosure exemption for churches means the public often cannot find out which kind it is dealing with.

## Accountability and the disclosure gap

The disclosure asymmetry may be the most practically significant piece of the puzzle. Secular nonprofits that receive tax exemptions are required to publish detailed financial information. That transparency is not incidental — it is the mechanism by which the public verifies that the tax benefit is justified. It allows journalists, researchers, and citizens to check whether an organisation claiming charitable status is actually doing charitable work.

Religious organisations in the United States (and in many other jurisdictions with comparable exemptions) are largely exempt from this requirement. The justification is the First Amendment — compelling religious organisations to disclose their finances might entangle the government in religious affairs, and the disclosure requirement itself might deter religious giving or association. These are not frivolous concerns. But the effect is a system in which the government grants a significant fiscal benefit while simultaneously waiving the accountability mechanism that justifies the benefit in every other context.

**Accountability-free subsidy** is a phrase that should trouble any serious secularist, and indeed any serious fiscal conservative. It creates a category of organisations that can accumulate property, income, and assets with no public scrutiny and no obligation to demonstrate that the exemption serves the public interest. The scandals that have periodically emerged from religious organisations — financial mismanagement, undisclosed salaries, assets shielded from creditors in abuse cases — have sometimes only come to light through investigative journalism rather than the routine disclosure that would catch such problems in a secular nonprofit.

## What a genuinely secular tax policy would look like

A principled secular approach to religious taxation does not require punishing religion. It requires applying the same standards to religious organisations that the state applies to everyone else. Several specific reforms follow from this:

The **disclosure requirements** that apply to all other tax-exempt nonprofits should apply to religious organisations equally. There is no compelling First Amendment reason why a church's finances must remain secret while a secular charity's do not. Religious belief is protected; financial opacity is not a religious practice.

**Charitable status should be conditional on charitable activity.** The current system in most jurisdictions does not require religious organisations to demonstrate that a meaningful portion of their revenue goes to public benefit activities rather than internal religious purposes. A secular framework would require the same showing from religious bodies that it requires from secular ones — not to evaluate the theological worth of the activity, but to verify that tax relief is flowing to genuine public goods.

The **parsonage exemption and equivalent housing benefits** for clergy are harder cases. A minister who lives modestly in accommodation tied to community service has a weak analogy to a senior executive with a multimillion-dollar housing allowance. But the current structure makes no such distinction. Capping the exemption at reasonable levels, consistent with the treatment of other nonprofit employees, would be both defensible and fiscally significant.

None of these reforms require the state to judge whether any religion is true, valuable, or worthwhile. They require only that the state apply consistent standards — which is precisely what a commitment to procedural secularism demands.

## The political difficulty

These reforms are politically difficult almost everywhere. Religious organisations are well-organised, span ideological coalitions, and have strong incentives to resist changes to their fiscal status. In the United States, any attempt to alter church tax treatment quickly collides with both the Religious Freedom Restoration Act jurisprudence and the political reality that religious voters are a significant constituency for both major parties.

But political difficulty is not the same as principled objection. The case for disclosure, consistent charitable standards, and the removal of religion-specific exemptions that have no secular parallel rests on premises — equal treatment, public accountability, the distinction between non-persecution and active subsidy — that are hard to reject in principle without also rejecting the broader liberal commitments that justify the exemptions in the first place.

Secularism, properly understood, is not a campaign against religion. It is a set of institutional commitments about how the state should relate to all citizens regardless of belief. One of those commitments is that the state should not direct public resources — including foregone tax revenue — toward specifically religious ends without the same accountability it demands of every other beneficiary of public subsidy. That is not hostility. It is consistency.
