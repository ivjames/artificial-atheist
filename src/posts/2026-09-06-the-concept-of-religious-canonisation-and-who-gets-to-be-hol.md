Declaring someone holy is one of the most consequential acts a religious institution can perform. It is also one of the least examined by outsiders, who tend to see it as an internal devotional matter of little analytical interest. That is a mistake.

## What canonisation actually does

At its simplest, canonisation is the formal recognition that a person is now, in some authoritative sense, a saint — a being of special proximity to the divine, worthy of veneration and capable of intercession. But this description obscures how much work the process does beyond its spiritual surface.

A canonised saint becomes a fixture of **authoritative memory**. Their life story is officially ratified; a particular reading of what they did and why is endorsed above competing readings. In the Roman Catholic tradition — the most bureaucratically elaborate canonisation system in existence — this means a historical claim is being made under papal authority: that this person is certainly in heaven. That is an extraordinary metaphysical assertion, and the institutional machinery built to support it reveals the pressures that bear on any claim to religious authority.

The process also creates **legal standing** in a religious sense. Parishes, religious orders, and national churches can claim a saint as patron. Churches can be named after them. Feast days enter the liturgical calendar. The saint becomes property of the institution in a way that living people never quite are.

## The evidentiary standards and what they reveal

The Catholic process is instructive precisely because it is explicit. A candidate must first be declared **Venerable**, then **Beatified**, and finally Canonised. Beatification generally requires one verified miracle; canonisation requires a second. The Vatican's Dicastery for the Causes of Saints employs medical consultants and theologians to examine miracle claims, which almost always involve alleged healings inexplicable by current medicine.

This procedure is revealing for skeptics because it is the one area where a religious institution formally submits its claims to something resembling external scrutiny. Medical experts are asked whether a recovery was scientifically explicable. The implicit standard is: if medicine cannot explain it, the gap is filled by divine intervention. This is a form of the argument from ignorance — **gaps in medical understanding become evidence for saintly intercession** — but the very fact that the church consults physicians at all shows an awareness that naked assertion is not enough, even within a community of believers.

Miracles can be downgraded or rejected. Causes can stall for centuries. The process produces false negatives (it almost certainly fails to canonise everyone a Catholic might consider genuinely holy) and is vulnerable to false positives driven by political pressure. Several causes have been fast-tracked for figures who served institutional interests: John Paul II's canonisation, completed in nine years, was controversially swift given unresolved questions about his handling of clerical abuse. The evidentiary rigour and the political pressures do not operate in separate compartments.

## Authority, geography, and the politics of holiness

Different religious traditions handle the question of sainthood very differently, and the contrast is illuminating. Eastern Orthodoxy canonises through synodal decision — the bishops of a local church or the pan-Orthodox council together — rather than through a papacy. This decentralisation means that the same person can be a saint in one Orthodox jurisdiction and not in another. Russia and Constantinople have, on several occasions, disagreed about whether a given figure merits veneration.

Protestantism largely abandoned formal canonisation at the Reformation, on the grounds that all believers are saints in the Pauline sense and that the veneration of particular individuals risked idolatry. Yet the impulse did not disappear. Protestant traditions created informal canons of exemplary figures — martyrs commemorated in calendars, reformers honoured with church names, civil rights leaders incorporated into liturgical memory by some Anglican and Lutheran churches. The Church of England's calendar now includes Martin Luther King Jr., Janani Luwum, and Oscar Romero. The process is less formalised but the function is identical: a community ratifies which lives it holds up as normative.

In Islam there is no formal canonisation process, yet the veneration of **wali** (friends of God, often translated as saints) is widespread in Sufi traditions and popular practice across South Asia, North Africa, and the Middle East. Wahhabi and Salafi reformers have attacked this practice as idolatrous innovation, sometimes literally — shrines have been destroyed in Saudi Arabia, Mali, and Libya on these grounds. The dispute is not simply theological; it is about who controls the definition of legitimate Islam, and holy figures become flashpoints because they concentrate localised, non-institutional religious authority that centralising reformers find threatening.

## Why the construction of holiness matters analytically

The social theorist Max Weber distinguished between **charismatic authority** — the raw, personal claim to special power — and **traditional authority**, which is institutionalised and routinised over time. Canonisation is precisely the mechanism by which charismatic figures are absorbed into traditional structures. The mystic who disrupted local religious norms becomes, after death and investigation, an officially approved exemplar who reinforces those same norms.

This routinisation is not cynical. The people running canonisation processes generally believe in what they are doing. But it is worth noting that the saints who survive the process are rarely the ones who most directly challenged institutional power. Thomas More was canonised; so was Thomas Becket. But both are remembered primarily for defying secular power, not ecclesiastical authority. The handful of figures canonised despite having criticised Rome — Joan of Arc comes to mind — tend to be rehabilitated in ways that fold their defiance into a narrative of misunderstood orthodoxy.

The pattern suggests that canonisation functions partly as **retrospective domestication**. The dangerous edges of a life are smoothed. What remains is an authorised version useful to the institution.

## The secular counterpart and what it shares

It would be convenient to treat canonisation as an exclusively religious phenomenon, but the structural logic has secular parallels that deserve acknowledgment. National pantheons — the Panthéon in Paris, Westminster Abbey, the Capitol's Statuary Hall — perform similar work. A life is officially ratified; a particular interpretation is endorsed; the figure is assigned intercessory cultural power (as a symbol, as an inspiration, as a rhetorical resource). The debates about whose statues belong in public squares, whose faces belong on currency, and whose names belong on institutions are debates about a secular canon.

The parallels do not make canonisation rational. They do suggest that the desire to officially designate exemplary figures is not a peculiarity of theological systems but a feature of how communities constitute and reproduce themselves. Understanding this does not require accepting any supernatural claims. It does require taking seriously that the institution is doing something real — something with consequences for living people — even when the metaphysical claims underwriting it are unfounded.

## What sainthood reveals about religious epistemology

Perhaps the most interesting feature of canonisation, from an epistemological standpoint, is its irreversibility in Catholic doctrine. Once the pope canonises, the declaration is held to be infallible — this person is in heaven. There is no mechanism for decanonisation on grounds of having made a mistake. There have been practical decanonisations: saints removed from the universal calendar because their historical existence could not be confirmed (St Christopher, St Philomena). But these are treated as failures of historical evidence, not failures of the canonisation process itself. If the evidence had been good, the declaration would have stood.

This structure is **unfalsifiable by design**. A negative miracle result can halt a cause; but a completed canonisation cannot be reversed by any subsequent evidence, because the verification is held to occur at a level — God's judgment — that is in principle inaccessible to later investigators. The epistemological closure is total once the process concludes. This is characteristic of many religious truth claims, but canonisation makes the structure unusually explicit: here is the moment at which the door closes on revision.

That is worth sitting with. Religious institutions often present their claims as humble — subject to mystery, open to development. Canonisation reveals a ceiling on that humility. At a defined moment, on the basis of a defined procedure, a claim is made that is permanently insulated from revision. Understanding where those moments are, and what interests they serve, is part of understanding how religious authority actually works.
