Naturalisation ceremonies are quiet constitutional moments. A person stands before a state official, recites an oath, and becomes a citizen. What that oath requires — and what values it implicitly or explicitly encodes — turns out to be one of the most revealing tests of what a secular state actually believes about itself.

## What oaths of citizenship typically ask

Most liberal democracies require some form of commitment from new citizens. The United States asks applicants to renounce prior allegiances and support the Constitution. The United Kingdom asks for loyalty to the monarch and respect for rights and freedoms. France, more explicitly, asks prospective citizens to affirm republican values: *liberté, égalité, fraternité*, and the principle of *laïcité* — France's particular brand of strict church-state separation.

The French case is instructive because it names secularism directly. A would-be citizen is not merely asked to obey French law; they are asked to affirm a philosophical orientation toward religion's place in public life. This is not trivial. A devout Catholic, Muslim, or Orthodox Jew who genuinely believes religious principles should guide legislation — a position held by millions of people throughout history and today — is being asked to subscribe, at least formally, to a view that contradicts their own.

Other countries are less explicit but encode the same tension implicitly. Commitments to "democratic values," "gender equality," or "the rule of law" can each conflict with the normative frameworks of certain religious communities. The question is not whether these values are defensible — most of them are — but whether demanding their affirmation as a condition of citizenship is consistent with the religious neutrality that secular states claim to practice.

## The integration test and its philosophical ambitions

Several European countries have extended this logic into pre-citizenship integration tests. The Netherlands, Germany, and Denmark have at various times administered civic integration exams that include questions designed to test tolerance of homosexuality, gender equality, or religious pluralism. Some versions of these tests asked applicants directly: do you find it acceptable that two men kiss in public? Would you send your daughter to a mixed-sex swimming class?

The stated purpose is reasonable: ensure that new citizens can function in a pluralistic society without imposing their values coercively on others. Nobody disputes that a state may legitimately require that citizens obey non-discrimination law. But integration tests often go further, probing attitudes rather than conduct. A person may privately find homosexuality sinful while never discriminating against gay colleagues or neighbours. The law, in a secular society, governs behaviour, not belief. When the naturalisation process begins testing the content of private belief, it starts to resemble exactly the kind of ideological conformity that secular liberalism is supposed to resist.

**The asymmetry here is striking.** A native-born citizen may hold any privately illiberal view without forfeiting citizenship. An immigrant holding the same view may be denied it. The secular state, which claims to be neutral on questions of comprehensive moral doctrine, is here selecting among comprehensive doctrines at the gate.

## The strongest case for value requirements

The strongest counterargument is not that the state is enforcing trivial preferences but that certain values are preconditions of the democratic project itself. If a person sincerely intends to use democratic institutions to impose theocratic rule — to vote for parties committed to replacing secular law with religious law — then admitting them to citizenship might, in aggregate, undermine the secular framework that makes free citizenship possible.

This is a genuine worry, not a paranoid one. There are democracies that have seen electoral movements aimed at dismantling the constitutional order. A state has a legitimate interest in its own continuity. The question is whether attitude tests are an effective or principled means of securing that interest.

They are probably neither. Attitude tests are easily gamed: an applicant who actually intends to work against liberal values simply lies, while a sincere believer who holds illiberal private views but has no intention of acting coercively against others fails. The test screens for candour more than for threat. Meanwhile, the principled objection stands: secular liberalism has traditionally been defined by its reluctance to impose a single moral worldview on citizens. The moment it conditions citizenship on endorsing such a worldview, it has departed from its own foundations.

## Dress codes at the ceremony and what they signal

A related question arises over religious dress at naturalisation ceremonies themselves. Several countries have debated whether a Muslim woman wearing a niqab, or a Sikh man wearing a turban, may complete the ceremony in that attire. France has argued that the niqab in particular violates the spirit of *laïcité* even in a private ceremony conducted before officials. Other countries, including Canada and the United Kingdom, have reached the opposite conclusion: that permitting religious dress at a citizenship ceremony demonstrates precisely the tolerance of difference that secular society promises.

These disputes are not merely aesthetic. They indicate what kind of secularism is on offer. **Procedural secularism** — the view that the state should remain neutral between religious and non-religious perspectives on matters where both can comply with common rules — is compatible with religious dress at a ceremony. **Assertive secularism** — the view that public civic space should be visibly free of religious expression — is not. France practises the latter. Most Anglophone democracies practise the former. Neither version is obviously correct, but they lead to very different treatment of the same new citizen.

## The republican paradox

France is the sharpest case because it is the most self-conscious. French republicanism holds that citizenship is universal and abstract: when you become French, you become a citizen first, with all particular identities — ethnic, religious, cultural — understood as private matters irrelevant to your public status. This is meant to be liberating. In theory, the son of North African immigrants and the descendant of Norman aristocrats stand before the republic identically.

In practice, the universalism cuts both ways. If your identity is supposed to be purely private, the state has no business asking about it. But if your religious commitments lead you to visible practices — wearing a headscarf, observing Friday prayers, seeking religious exemptions from otherwise general rules — the republican framework treats those commitments as a challenge to universalism rather than an expression of liberty. The person who holds no strong religious identity experiences the republic as neutral. The person whose religion shapes their visible conduct experiences it as demanding.

This is not a bug unique to French republicanism; it is a structural feature of any secularism that mistakes the absence of visible religion for genuine neutrality. When the default is the secular mode of life, making that default invisible is itself a philosophical choice. Requiring new citizens to affirm it without acknowledging that it is a choice — one among several legitimate options — is a form of bad faith, even when the values being affirmed are defensible on independent grounds.

## What a more consistent secularism would look like

A more principled approach would distinguish clearly between three things: conduct, expression, and belief.

A secular state has clear authority to regulate **conduct**: new citizens must obey laws against discrimination, violence, and coercion, regardless of religious justification. No exemption from the legal order is granted on religious grounds when that order reflects a genuinely compelling state interest.

**Expression** is harder. Permitting religious dress in civic spaces costs the state nothing and signals real tolerance. Banning it imposes a cost on some citizens that others never bear. The asymmetry is difficult to justify on neutral grounds.

**Belief** — the content of private moral and metaphysical conviction — is outside the state's proper authority. A secularism that probes or conditions entry on the content of private belief has overstepped. It has become not the absence of a state religion but the imposition of a secular ideology as a functional substitute for one.

Naturalisation ceremonies could express genuine secular values more honestly by making this distinction explicit: you are being asked to commit to the rule of law and equal treatment under it, not to adopt any particular view of how human beings ought to understand the cosmos or organise their private moral lives. That is a meaningful commitment. It is also a more defensible one — and one that a person of almost any faith tradition can make without self-betrayal.

The alternative — a secularism that conditions belonging on ideological conformity — ends up resembling what it claims to oppose. It replaces a religious test for citizenship with a metaphysical one of its own. That should unsettle anyone who cares about what secular governance is actually for.
