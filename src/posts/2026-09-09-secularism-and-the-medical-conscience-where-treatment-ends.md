Secular governance promises neutrality between worldviews. That promise is tested most sharply when a worldview belongs to someone who holds power over another person's body.

## The structure of the conflict

Medical conscience claims arise when a licensed professional refuses to provide, refer for, or participate in a legal procedure because doing so conflicts with their religious or moral convictions. The procedures at issue include abortion, emergency contraception, vasectomy, tubal ligation, physician-assisted dying, gender-affirming hormone therapy, and in some cases blood transfusion and certain vaccines. The refusers are pharmacists, nurses, anaesthetists, and physicians — people whose licences are granted by the state and whose services are often not easily replaced.

The conflict is real on both sides. A clinician forced to perform an act they consider gravely wrong suffers a serious violation of integrity. A patient denied time-sensitive care — an emergency contraceptive that loses efficacy by the hour, a lethal prescription in a jurisdiction with a months-long wait — suffers a concrete harm. Secularism cannot dissolve this conflict by fiat. What it can do is provide a framework for deciding whose claim takes priority in which circumstances, and why.

## What conscience protections actually do

**Conscience clauses** in healthcare law are not exotic. The United States federal Church Amendments (1973) were the first significant example, protecting clinicians who refused to perform abortions or sterilisations from employment discrimination. Most liberal democracies now have some version of such protection, whether in statute, professional codes, or both. Their existence reflects a genuine secular insight: the state should not conscript professionals into acts they find morally intolerable any more than it should conscript soldiers into wars they find unconscionable.

But conscience protections in medicine have a structural feature that distinguishes them from most other conscience claims: the person exercising the conscience is not withdrawing from a shared public burden — they are refusing to serve a specific individual who has come to them for help. A conscientious objector to military service steps back from a collective project. A clinician who refuses to prescribe contraception turns away a patient standing at the counter. The asymmetry matters. The soldier's refusal distributes a burden across other citizens. The clinician's refusal concentrates a harm on a single, often vulnerable person.

## The referral problem

Conscience regimes typically resolve this asymmetry with a referral requirement: the refusing clinician must direct the patient to a willing provider. This looks like a clean solution. In practice it often is not.

Referral requirements fail in at least three situations. First, in rural or underserved areas, there may be no willing provider within a reasonable distance — particularly for procedures that are both legal and rare enough that few local practitioners offer them. Second, some religious objectors regard referral as morally equivalent to participation: if directing someone to an abortionist makes you causally complicit in the abortion, the referral requirement merely outsources rather than resolves the conflict. Third, for time-sensitive interventions, a delay of even 24–48 hours while a patient locates another provider can be medically significant.

The third case is where secular principle is clearest. No conscience framework that calls itself neutral can permit a religious belief to impose a medical cost that falls entirely on the patient and not at all on the objector. The emergency department pharmacist who refuses to dispense a post-rape emergency contraceptive on a Sunday evening, when no other pharmacy is open, is not exercising a private belief — they are making a coercive intervention in someone else's medical care. Secularism's job is to notice this.

## The institutional versus individual distinction

One underused conceptual tool in these debates is the distinction between **individual conscience** and **institutional conscience**. An individual clinician has a first-person moral identity that grounds a genuine conscience claim. A hospital corporation, a pharmacy chain, or a publicly funded health authority does not have a conscience in any analogous sense — it has a policy and, in some cases, a religious affiliation.

When a Catholic hospital network acquires the only maternity ward in a region and then refuses to perform tubal ligations, patients lose access not because of one clinician's sincere belief but because of a corporate policy applied system-wide. The conscience at stake is institutional and abstract; the harm to patients is individual and concrete. Several secular democracies have begun to distinguish these cases in law. France prohibits hospitals from refusing to perform legal abortions even if individual staff members object. Australia's state of Victoria requires that private hospitals receiving public funding offer termination services, regardless of religious affiliation.

This is the right direction. Extending conscience protection to institutions conflates the genuine moral standing of a person with the market position of an organisation. Secularism is consistent with protecting the former. It has no good reason to protect the latter, especially where public funding is involved.

## The limits of symmetry arguments

Defenders of broad conscience protections often invoke symmetry: just as secular clinicians should not be forced to perform religious rituals, religious clinicians should not be forced to perform procedures their faith condemns. The analogy sounds fair. It collapses under examination.

No state in a secular democracy requires clinicians to perform religious rituals. The procedures at issue — abortion, contraception, assisted dying — are not secular equivalents of religious acts. They are legal medical interventions with established clinical indications. The asymmetry is not between secular and religious values; it is between a patient's access to legal healthcare and a provider's preference not to deliver it. Calling that preference a conscience claim does not change what it is structurally: a third party imposing a restriction on someone else's medical options.

This does not mean conscience claims deserve no weight. It means that symmetry arguments cannot be used to place them above patient welfare by definition. The question of where conscience ends and patient abandonment begins is a practical and ethical one, not one that symmetry settles.

## What a secular framework actually requires

A coherent secular approach to medical conscience would have several components.

It would protect individual refusal for non-emergency procedures where the patient can be referred to a willing provider without meaningful delay or cost. A GP who will not prescribe the contraceptive pill may decline, provided they inform the patient clearly and provide a prompt referral. This respects genuine conscience without harming patients who have realistic alternatives.

It would require participation in emergencies without exception. If a patient presents in a condition where a particular intervention is immediately necessary to prevent serious harm or death, no conscience claim can override the duty of care. This is already the standard in most jurisdictions, though enforcement varies.

It would deny institutional conscience claims by publicly funded bodies. Hospitals, clinics, and pharmacies that receive state money operate under a public function that cannot be subordinated to sectarian policy. A faith-based hospital may retain its religious character in its chaplaincy and its culture; it may not use that character to restrict the legal medical options of patients who have no other nearby facility.

It would require transparency from individual objectors. Patients should be told at the point of registration — not at the moment of need — which services a practice does not provide and why. Hidden refusal imposed at a moment of vulnerability is not a conscience claim; it is a trap.

Finally, it would not require moral endorsement. A secular framework asks clinicians to provide legal services, not to approve of them. A pharmacist who dispenses emergency contraception while personally opposing it is not being asked to abandon their faith. They are being asked to perform the function for which they are licensed. The distinction between performing an act and endorsing it is basic enough that demanding it of professionals is not unreasonable.

## Why this is a secularism question and not just a bioethics one

Medical conscience debates are often treated as purely bioethical — a matter for professional codes, hospital committees, and clinical ethics consultants. That framing is too narrow. These disputes are also about the relationship between state licensing, public function, and religious exemption.

The state licences clinicians. It grants them authority to prescribe controlled substances, to perform surgery, to certify death. In return, it expects them to operate within a framework of legal obligations that applies regardless of faith. When a religious exemption carves out a class of legal acts that particular clinicians may refuse, the state is effectively deputising religious communities to restrict access to its own legal services. That is not neutrality. It is a quiet subsidy to particular worldviews at the expense of the patients those worldviews happen to disfavour.

Secularism is not anti-religious. It does not tell clinicians what to believe, what to pray, or how to live outside the clinical encounter. What it says is this: the licence is public, the patient is entitled to legal care, and faith is not a veto over another person's body. That is a position worth defending — carefully, without contempt for genuine conscience, but without flinching from what is actually at stake.
