When a country repeals its blasphemy law, secularists typically mark it as a victory. Ireland did it in 2018, following a referendum. India continues to debate the colonial-era provisions still on its books. The celebrations and the debates share an assumption: that the law is the problem. The reality is more complicated.

## What blasphemy laws actually prohibit

Classic blasphemy law, in its common law form, was not primarily about protecting God. God was assumed to be able to look after himself. The law protected the established church as a social institution and, by extension, the civil order that institution underwrote. The 1922 English case *R v Gott* — the last successful blasphemy prosecution in England — convicted a man for selling pamphlets mocking Christ, not because the court feared metaphysical offense, but because "vilification of Christianity" was held to threaten public order.

This framing matters. **Blasphemy law was always, at bottom, a public order law with a theological dressing.** When states stripped out the theological dressing, they rarely asked whether the public order rationale had also gone. In many cases it had not.

Modern successor provisions often include "religious hatred" or "incitement to religious offense" statutes. Ireland replaced its blasphemy offense not with nothing, but with the existing prohibition on incitement to hatred, which covers religion alongside race and sexual orientation. Germany's §166 StGB, the *Beschimpfung von Bekenntnissen*, forbids disturbing public peace by insulting religious beliefs. Pakistan's infamous provisions under §295 were themselves introduced by a colonial administration and strengthened by Zia ul-Haq in 1986 — they remain, and they remain lethal in practice. The formal repeal in countries like Ireland changes the symbolic register without necessarily changing how communities enforce conformity on religious speech.

## The gap between law and enforcement

Even where blasphemy laws have been formally removed, the silence they trained into public life does not simply evaporate. Social enforcement of religious deference operates through mechanisms the law neither creates nor easily destroys: professional ostracism, family pressure, platform removal, mob action, and the quiet editorial judgment that a joke at the expense of Christianity is publishable where the same joke targeting Islam is not, or vice versa depending on the readership.

The secularist argument for repeal rests on the view that the state should not be in the business of policing religious reputation. That argument is sound. But it says nothing about the informal pressures that produce the same result without a statute. A country can have no blasphemy law and still have a de facto blasphemy norm, enforced by the social cost of transgression rather than by courts.

This is not a reason to keep blasphemy laws. It is a reason to notice what repeal does and does not accomplish, and to be honest about the further work required.

## The asymmetry problem

One of the clearest complications in any secular approach to blasphemy is the **asymmetry of sensitivity across religious communities**. In pluralist democracies, the political pressure to extend special protection against offense tracks the political organisation and perceived vulnerability of different groups. The result is not a neutral secular space but a landscape of uneven informal protections.

Some European states have in practice prosecuted speech targeting Islam more aggressively than equivalent speech targeting Christianity under their public order or hate speech frameworks, because the political context of anti-Muslim discrimination makes the former easier to frame as incitement. Others have done the opposite, treating older Christian majorities as deserving symbolic deference. Neither approach is genuinely secular. Both involve the state making a judgment about which religious community currently needs protection, which is itself a quasi-theological political act.

A consistent secular position would hold that no religious belief system, as a set of propositions and practices, enjoys protection from criticism, satire, or mockery. Individuals may be protected from harassment and violence — that protection applies universally and requires no religious classification. But the ideas themselves, including religious ideas, must remain open to challenge. The difficulty is that modern hate speech law, however well-intentioned, tends to blur this distinction because it is easier to legislate against offense than to draw a clean line between attacking a belief and attacking its adherents.

## How secular states have tried to draw the line

The French model is instructive. France abolished blasphemy as an offense in the mainland in 1881, though Alsace-Moselle retained the old German provisions until a court finally struck them in 2017. The French republican tradition holds that *laïcité* protects the right to criticise religion in terms that would be unacceptable in many Anglophone democracies. The *Charlie Hebdo* attacks in 2015 briefly made this a global controversy: was the magazine's satire protected expression or reckless provocation?

The French legal and political establishment's answer was clear — it was protected expression, full stop. The secular principle does not contain an exception for satire that a religious community finds mortally offensive. Critics, including some within the French left, argued that the principle, while formally correct, was being applied selectively, with Muslim sensibilities more often on the receiving end of aggressive satire than Christian or Jewish ones. That criticism may or may not be empirically accurate, but it points to something real: **formal legal equality does not guarantee equal treatment in the cultural practice of criticism**.

The American approach has been different. The First Amendment removes blasphemy law entirely and, largely, prevents hate speech law of the European kind from taking hold at the federal level. The result is a more consistent free expression norm, but no less social pressure. American publishers, platforms, and broadcasters routinely self-censor on religious topics not because they fear prosecution but because they fear audience reaction, advertiser withdrawal, or accusations of bigotry. The silence exists; only the mechanism differs.

## What genuine secular neutrality requires

If a secular state is serious about neutrality regarding religion, it has to resist two opposite temptations: the temptation to protect religious belief from criticism because believers feel wounded, and the temptation to single out religion for aggressive treatment because it is not a protected characteristic in the way that race is. Neither position is neutral.

**True neutrality on religious speech means applying exactly the standards used for any other domain of contested belief.** Political ideologies, scientific consensus, philosophical systems, and historical narratives are all subject to vigorous public criticism, satire, and even mockery. The state does not protect Marxism from caricature or the theory of evolution from mockery, even though adherents of each may feel genuine distress at being mocked. Religious beliefs should occupy the same position — not singled out for persecution, but not immunised against challenge.

This is uncomfortable for everyone. Believers find it cold. Some secularists find it inconveniently even-handed because it rules out the kind of deliberately provocative anti-religious satire that functions more as tribal signalling than genuine criticism. And it places pressure on hate speech frameworks that have tried to protect religious identity without acknowledging how much of that protection bleeds into protecting religious doctrine.

## What remains after the law goes

The underlying question blasphemy law was always meant to resolve is: can a political community hold together when its members fundamentally disagree about what is sacred? The historical answer was to enforce agreement, or at least silence, by law. The secular answer is to dissolve the legal enforcement and rely on the political culture of free inquiry to do the harder work.

That culture is not self-sustaining. It requires a press willing to exercise the rights it has. It requires courts willing to resist popular pressure to protect feelings dressed up as public order. It requires citizens who distinguish between the right not to be harassed and the desire not to be challenged. And it requires secularists who apply their own principles consistently rather than selectively.

Formal repeal of blasphemy provisions is a necessary step. The Irish referendum, the Alsace-Moselle ruling, the ongoing legislative reform debates in South Asian democracies — these matter. But they mark the beginning of the secular project on religious speech, not its completion. The social architecture that gives blasphemy norms their staying power does not appear in any statute, and it will not disappear when one is removed. Naming that architecture clearly is the next task.
