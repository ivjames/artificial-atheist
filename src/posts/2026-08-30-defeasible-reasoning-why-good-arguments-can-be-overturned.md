Formal logic deals in certainty: if the premises are true and the argument is valid, the conclusion must follow. But most of the reasoning people actually do — in courts, in medicine, in everyday life — works differently. New evidence can arrive and legitimately overturn a conclusion that was previously well-supported. This is **defeasible reasoning**, and understanding it changes how you evaluate arguments and assess belief.

## What defeasible reasoning is

An argument is **defeasible** if its conclusion can be defeated by additional premises even when the original premises remain true. Compare two arguments:

1. All mammals are warm-blooded. Whales are mammals. Therefore whales are warm-blooded.
2. Birds fly. Tweety is a bird. Therefore Tweety flies.

The first is deductively valid — there is no coherent world in which the premises hold and the conclusion fails. The second feels just as natural, but it collapses the moment you learn Tweety is a penguin. The premise "birds fly" was never meant as an exceptionless universal law; it was a generalisation carrying implicit qualifications. Most of our everyday generalisations are like that.

The philosopher John Pollock introduced the vocabulary of **undercutting defeaters** and **rebutting defeaters** to sort out what kind of defeat is happening. A rebutting defeater directly contradicts the conclusion — learning Tweety is a penguin directly contradicts "Tweety flies." An undercutting defeater attacks the inferential link between evidence and conclusion without denying either. If you see red flashing lights in a dark room and infer that there is a fire engine outside, learning that a photographer is using a strobe light undercuts the inference without saying there is definitely no fire engine. The distinction matters because the appropriate epistemic response differs: a rebuttal demands you revise the conclusion, an undercutter demands you suspend it.

## Why deductive logic alone is not enough

The picture of rational belief that dominated early analytic philosophy treated good reasoning as ideally deductive. Evidence confirms or disconfirms hypotheses, and belief tracks the balance of that confirmation. Induction was awkward, but at least it pointed in the same direction as the evidence.

Defeasible reasoning is trickier. It does not fail to be deductive — it actively licenses conclusions while preserving their revisability. Legal scholars noticed this early. A defendant's known presence at a crime scene is good evidence of guilt, but evidence that they were there to purchase groceries, combined with CCTV footage showing someone else committing the act, defeats the inference entirely. At no point was the original evidence false; it just stopped being the whole story.

This is why **non-monotonic logic** was developed in artificial intelligence research during the 1980s. Classical (monotonic) logic has the property that adding premises never removes conclusions: if you can derive C from {A, B}, you can still derive C from {A, B, D}. Real reasoning is non-monotonic. A doctor who infers pneumonia from fever and chest pain and then receives a negative sputum culture must revise that inference, even though the original symptoms have not disappeared.

## The epistemological stakes

Defeasibility has direct consequences for how one should think about the justification of beliefs. The **prima facie justification** model — associated with Pollock and later developed by epistemologists like John Horty — holds that evidence can justify a belief without that justification being indefeasible. You are rational to believe P on the basis of E even if you know that some possible undiscovered defeater could later overturn P. Rationality is not the same as certainty.

This is the normal condition of empirical inquiry. A geologist reading rock strata to date a formation is reasoning defeasibly: the inference from observed layering patterns to "these layers took approximately 10,000 years to form" is well-supported by background theory, but it is also the kind of claim that could, in principle, be revised if anomalous processes were discovered. The revisability does not make the geologist irrational for holding the belief now. It just makes the geologist a scientist.

The opposite error — demanding that beliefs be indefeasible before they are accepted — leads to a paralysing form of scepticism. If no conclusion is acceptable until no possible defeater exists, virtually nothing qualifies. Defeasible justification is not a concession to weakness; it is how finite reasoners living in an uncertain world can function responsibly.

## Defeaters and the structure of disagreement

One under-appreciated application of this framework is to disagreement. When two people reason from the same initial evidence to different conclusions, it is tempting to say that one has simply made an error. But often they hold different background beliefs that act as defeaters to each other's inferences. Neither party may be irrational given their respective total evidence sets; the disagreement is partly a matter of which defeaters each has encountered.

Consider debates about the reliability of eyewitness testimony. A juror without knowledge of cognitive psychology might infer that a confident, detailed eyewitness account strongly supports the accused's presence at the scene. A juror who knows the literature on memory malleability and the misinformation effect has an undercutting defeater for that inference: not evidence that the witness is lying, but evidence that even sincere, confident testimony is unreliable under specific conditions. The second juror is not simply "more sceptical" in some blanket sense — they have a specific defeater that the first lacks.

This matters for how disagreements should be conducted. Rather than asking only "is your conclusion true?" the better question is often "what would defeat your inference, and have you encountered it?" This turns adversarial debate into something more like mutual defeater-disclosure.

## Defeasibility and religious and philosophical arguments

Many of the arguments people find most persuasive in philosophy of religion, ethics, and metaphysics are defeasible rather than deductive, and treating them as if they were deductive creates confusion on both sides.

Take a **cosmological argument** at its most careful: it moves from observations about contingent existence to a conclusion about a necessary being. Even granting the premises, the inference involves steps that carry implicit qualifications — about what "explanation" requires, about whether the regress truly cannot terminate without a necessary being, about whether a necessary being must have the properties attributed to God. Each of those steps is a potential site for an undercutting defeater. A sceptic who introduces one is not simply refusing to engage; they are doing exactly what the logic of defeasible reasoning requires.

The same applies going the other direction. An atheist who argues from the existence of suffering to the non-existence of God is making a defeasible inference. Theists who respond with theodicies — arguments that the suffering is compatible with divine purposes — are introducing potential defeaters. The question is whether those defeaters are themselves well-supported or whether they are ad hoc manoeuvres. Defeasible reasoning does not license any response; it requires that the defeater itself meet evidential standards.

**Burden of proof** questions look different through this lens too. The common framing treats burden of proof as a threshold question: who must prove what before the other side is obliged to respond? The defeasibility framework suggests a more dynamic picture. Both sides are continuously accumulating and producing defeaters, and the rational conclusion at any moment is whatever best survives the current set. That is a messier picture than "the theist must prove, full stop," but it is more accurate to how careful inquiry actually proceeds.

## What this demands of reasoners

Defeasible reasoning places real obligations on anyone who takes it seriously. You must not only track your current reasons but actively seek potential defeaters — evidence or arguments that, if true, would undercut your inferences. This is not the same as motivated scepticism, where you apply high standards only to conclusions you dislike. It means being genuinely open to the question: what would it look like if I were wrong about this, and have I actually checked?

It also means being honest about the weight of defeaters you have encountered. Someone who dismisses a rebutting defeater — direct counter-evidence — as "just one study" or "an edge case" is not engaging with the logic of defeasible reasoning. Defeaters deserve proportionate weight, which means sometimes they are devastating and sometimes they are weak. The argument is about that weight, not about whether defeat is possible in principle.

Finally, defeasibility is a reason to hold conclusions with appropriate tentativeness — not performative uncertainty, but a genuine internal acknowledgement that conclusions are held on current evidence and are open to revision. That is not intellectual weakness. It is the only honest response to the fact that reasoners are always working with incomplete information in a world that keeps producing more.

The logician who demands airtight deductive proof for every important claim will be idle much of their life. The reasoner who understands defeasibility can engage seriously with evidence, track the movement of defeaters, and update beliefs as the full picture develops — which is precisely what careful thinking about hard questions requires.
