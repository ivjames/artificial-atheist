Public broadcasting sits at an unusual intersection: funded by everyone, accountable to democratic institutions, yet expected to remain editorially independent. When the audience is religiously diverse — or substantially non-religious — decisions about how much airtime to give faith, whose theology to platform, and whether to treat religious claims like any other factual claim become genuinely hard. These are not marginal questions. They recur every budget cycle, every scheduling review, every time a producer has to decide whether a creationist deserves a seat opposite an evolutionary biologist.

## The structural problem with "balance"

Most public broadcasters operate under some version of a **due impartiality** requirement. The BBC's editorial guidelines, for instance, require due weight to a range of significant views — but explicitly note that not all positions are entitled to equal weight. In practice, however, religion occupies an unusual niche. Because faith is treated simultaneously as a matter of private conviction and as a cultural heritage, broadcasters often apply a softened standard: religious claims receive airtime that comparable non-evidential claims would not.

This creates a structural asymmetry. A documentary claiming that dowsing rods detect underground water would be framed sceptically or not commissioned at all. A documentary exploring the claim that prayer heals illness will frequently be framed with deference, on the grounds that faith is personal and communities must not be offended. The asymmetry is not always conscious, but it is consistent enough to constitute an editorial policy by default.

The fairest reading of due impartiality would apply the same scrutiny to religious empirical claims that it applies to any other. Where a claim is testable — intercessory prayer, the age of the earth, the efficacy of exorcism — editorial standards should treat it as testable. Where a claim is metaphysical and untestable, it is appropriately covered as belief rather than fact. Public broadcasters rarely hold that line with consistency.

## Religious programming as a legacy arrangement

Many public broadcasters carry dedicated religious programming as a condition of their founding charters or licences, agreed during periods when near-universal nominal Christianity made the decision uncontroversial. The BBC's *Thought for the Day* — a daily two-to-three minute slot on Radio 4's flagship morning news programme — has been broadcast since 1970 and is explicitly restricted to contributors from religious traditions. Secular humanist and atheist contributors are excluded by policy, despite repeated campaigns and an audience whose non-religious proportion has grown from marginal to substantial over that period.

The argument for keeping such arrangements intact is partly historical (the slot predates today's diversity), partly cultural (religion remains socially significant even where personal belief has declined), and partly practical (removing it would trigger disproportionate controversy). None of these arguments is quite an argument on the merits. They are, collectively, an argument from inertia.

The counterargument — that dedicated religious programming serves communities who would otherwise find their central concerns absent from public discourse — is real and deserves engagement. Religious minorities in particular can find public broadcasting culturally hostile or indifferent; a guaranteed slot represents institutional recognition. But this argument supports guaranteed access for minority communities, not a blanket exemption for religious content from the ordinary standards that apply to everything else. Those are different things, and conflating them has allowed legacy arrangements to persist well past their democratic justification.

## Who counts as a religious broadcaster

A related problem concerns which faith traditions qualify for coverage. Public broadcasters in Western Europe with Christian-heritage structures routinely cover Christianity — Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox — in detail, devote moderate coverage to Islam and Judaism, and provide token or negligible coverage of Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism, and newer religious movements. This is partly a reflection of demographic distribution, but the disproportion often exceeds what demographics would justify. 

More pointed still is the question of what counts as a religion for broadcasting purposes. **Scientology** has pursued broadcasting coverage in multiple countries on grounds of religious equality; several national regulators have had to develop criteria distinguishing recognised religions from organisations using the legal category of religion for other purposes. France's Conseil supérieur de l'audiovisuel and the UK's Ofcom have both faced litigation on these questions. The decisions reached are rarely principled in a philosophically satisfying way; they tend instead to reflect sociological judgments about institutional longevity and social embeddedness — which in practice advantages older traditions.

Secular and humanist organisations present a different challenge. They are not religions, but they address many of the same existential and ethical questions, represent a growing demographic, and often feel misrepresented or absent from public discourse. Whether they are owed equivalent access to religious programming slots, or whether access should be reframed around worldviews rather than religions, is actively debated in several European jurisdictions. Belgium has moved furthest in this direction, recognising secular humanism as a funded worldview alongside recognised religions in its constitutional settlement — a model that puts secular perspectives on formal institutional parity.

## The editorial independence problem

Public broadcasters face political pressure from governments that are rarely, in practice, religiously neutral. In Poland, the government that took power in 2015 transformed public broadcasting into an instrument of cultural conservatism that included explicit alignment with the Catholic Church's positions on abortion, LGBTQ+ rights, and European integration. In Israel, the evolution of public broadcasting has reflected the shifting power of religious parties within coalition governments. In the United States, where public broadcasting is federally funded but operates with independent governance, congressional pressure has periodically targeted programming perceived as insufficiently respectful of religious sentiment.

The pattern is consistent across contexts: governments with strong religious constituencies use their leverage over funding and board appointments to tilt editorial decisions toward religious deference. This is not the same as a secular government imposing irreligion — and the asymmetry matters. A broadcaster that refuses to run a programme critical of faith on grounds that it will offend audiences is exercising a form of self-censorship that has no equivalent in the treatment of political, scientific, or commercial subjects.

Genuine editorial independence requires that religious institutions receive the same scrutiny as other powerful social institutions. That means investigative journalism about clerical abuse is not balanced by devotional programming about the beauty of the liturgy. It means that a church's lobbying campaign against legislation is reported as lobbying, not as pastoral guidance. It means that the financial arrangements of prosperity gospel ministries are appropriate subjects for documentary journalism. Some public broadcasters meet this standard; many do not, and the gap is most visible when religion intersects with political power.

## The non-religious audience

Census data across most European countries and the Anglophone world now shows that the proportion of adults identifying as having no religion ranges from roughly 25 percent in the United States to over 50 percent in several northern European states. This is the fastest-growing demographic category in most surveys of religious identity. Yet programming explicitly oriented toward this audience — not atheist polemic, but simply content that addresses ethical, existential, and meaning-related questions from a non-religious standpoint — remains uncommon on public broadcasters.

The practical effect is that public broadcasting, funded by universal taxation or licence fees, provides systematic cultural subsidy to religious perspectives while the non-religious audience receives either nothing comparable or coverage of their views through the lens of controversy: the atheist who debates the bishop, the humanist who appears on a panel about assisted dying. The non-religious are present as interlocutors, rarely as protagonists of their own cultural programming.

This is not a trivial point. Public broadcasting's legitimacy rests on the claim that it serves the whole public. A BBC or an ABC that consistently frames questions of meaning, death, ethics, and community through religious categories is, in practice, not serving a large and growing portion of the population it is funded to represent. The fix does not require anti-religious programming — it requires that human-scale questions be addressed through a range of frameworks, of which religious ones are several among many.

## What a genuinely neutral approach looks like

Neutrality in public broadcasting is not the same as giving equal time to every position regardless of its evidentiary standing. It is a procedural commitment to applying consistent standards — of evidence, of scrutiny, and of representation. Applied to religion, that means several things in practice.

Empirical claims made by religious organisations should meet the same evidential bar as empirical claims made by anyone else. Metaphysical and devotional content can be clearly labelled as such and covered as part of the culture, the same way literature or music programming covers works that presuppose a worldview the broadcaster does not officially endorse. Dedicated faith-based slots should either be opened to secular worldviews on equivalent terms or explicitly reframed as minority-community access programming with consistent criteria for who qualifies. And investigative journalism about religious institutions should proceed without the special reluctance that currently attaches to it in many editorial cultures.

None of this requires hostility to religion. The BBC at its best — when it covers the sociological significance of the hajj, the theological dispute behind a schism, or the experience of leaving a closed religious community — treats religion with the same intelligent seriousness it brings to any other human phenomenon. The problem is that this standard is applied inconsistently, and the inconsistency almost always runs in the same direction: toward deference and away from scrutiny. A genuinely secular public broadcaster does not treat religion with contempt. It treats religion as it treats everything else — which, from the perspective of many religious institutions, feels like the same thing.
