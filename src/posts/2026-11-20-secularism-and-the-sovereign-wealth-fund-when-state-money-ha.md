Sovereign wealth funds are among the most powerful financial actors on earth. Norway's Government Pension Fund Global owns roughly 1.5% of all listed equities worldwide. Abu Dhabi's ADIA manages an estimated $900 billion. These are not banks or charities — they are pools of public money deployed to secure a nation's long-term wealth. Most discussions of them focus on returns, geopolitics, or transparency. Almost none ask the question that sits quietly underneath: what happens when funds governed by religious principles become deeply embedded in secular financial systems?

## What Islamic finance actually requires

The clearest case is **Islamic finance**, the body of rules derived from Sharia that governs money in several Gulf sovereign wealth funds and in state-linked institutions across Malaysia, Saudi Arabia, and Kuwait. Its core prohibitions are well known in financial circles but rarely explained clearly elsewhere.

**Riba** — usually translated as "interest" — is forbidden. So is **gharar**, which covers excessive uncertainty or speculative contracts. **Maisir**, gambling or wagering, is likewise prohibited. These rules are not incidental; they are rooted in Quranic injunctions that treat money as a medium of exchange rather than a commodity to be rented.

In practice this means Islamic funds cannot hold conventional bonds, cannot invest in companies deriving revenue above a certain threshold from alcohol, pork products, conventional financial services, pornography, tobacco, or weapons, and must structure returns as profit-sharing arrangements rather than interest payments. Sharia supervisory boards — panels of Islamic scholars — certify compliance. These scholars have genuine authority: they can veto investment decisions.

This is not a marginal phenomenon. The **Qatar Investment Authority**, which owns significant stakes in Heathrow Airport, Volkswagen, and several major European banks, operates under Islamic finance principles. The **Kuwait Investment Authority**, the world's oldest sovereign wealth fund, is similarly structured. When a secular European government negotiates infrastructure investment with these funds, it is, whether it acknowledges it or not, dealing with capital whose deployment conditions are set partly by religious law.

## The secular state as indirect participant

The more awkward question is not what religious funds do internally, but what happens when secular states benefit from them or align with them.

Consider pension systems. Several European countries hold positions in funds that themselves hold positions in Gulf sovereign wealth funds, creating chain ownership where the ultimate source of capital and its governing logic are religious. The Norwegian oil fund, itself scrupulously secular in its ethical guidelines, competes and sometimes co-invests with funds whose ethical exclusions are set by scholars rather than parliamentary committees.

Co-investment is the sharper case. When the **UK Infrastructure Bank** or a French state development institution co-invests alongside a Sharia-compliant fund in a renewable energy project, it implicitly accepts the terms on which that capital is deployed. If the Islamic fund excludes certain financial instruments that the secular institution would normally use, accommodations must be made. The secular party either accepts constraints it did not set, or it structures the deal so that the religious constraints apply only to the Islamic fund's tranche — a clean separation that is easier to describe than to enforce in practice.

The question of **reciprocity** matters here. Secular states routinely require that foreign investment meet domestic legal standards — anti-discrimination law, environmental regulation, labour rules. They do not typically require that investors' internal governance be secular. That may be entirely correct: a fund governed by religious principles is not thereby violating the secular state's laws any more than a Quaker-founded bank violates them by refusing to invest in arms. But the asymmetry is worth noticing. The secular state applies its law to the investment; it does not scrutinise the theology behind the investor.

## The problem of Sharia-compliant sovereign borrowing

A sharper case arises when a secular state wants to borrow money from Islamic investors. Several non-Muslim-majority countries have issued **sukuk** — Islamic bonds, structured as asset-backed profit-sharing certificates rather than conventional debt — specifically to attract Gulf capital.

The United Kingdom issued its first sovereign sukuk in 2014. Luxembourg, Hong Kong, and South Africa followed. These instruments are not illegal, and they raise money at competitive rates. But their structure requires that returns be tied to identifiable government assets, that the religious screening criteria be satisfied, and that a Sharia board certify the instrument. When the British government issues a sukuk backed on government property assets, it is structuring a public financial instrument so that it passes religious review. No Sharia board, no sukuk, no money.

This is not a scandal. It is a pragmatic financial decision, and secular governments make analogous accommodations constantly — issuing green bonds whose criteria are set by external standards bodies, for instance. But the analogy only goes so far. Green bond standards are set by bodies whose authority is technical and revisable. Sharia certification derives its authority from a tradition of religious scholarship. When a secular state's borrowing instruments must satisfy that scholarship, it has introduced a religious veto — however limited in scope — into public finance.

## Ethical exclusions: convergence and divergence

It is tempting to dissolve the problem by pointing to convergence. Islamic finance excludes tobacco and arms manufacturers; so does the Norwegian fund on ethical grounds. If the exclusion lists overlap, does the religious basis of one of them matter?

It matters in two ways. First, the **reasoning** matters for how the rules evolve. The Norwegian fund's exclusions are determined by an ethics council that applies publicly stated, revisable criteria, subject to parliamentary oversight. If new evidence emerges that a previously acceptable industry causes serious harm, the criteria can be updated through a transparent process. Sharia screening criteria are updated too — scholars debate, for example, whether social media companies meet the standards — but the ultimate authority is textual and scholarly rather than democratic and empirical. These are genuinely different epistemic frameworks.

Second, the **divergences** are real and consequential. Islamic finance excludes conventional banking and insurance, which secular ethical funds generally do not. It permits investment in real estate development that might displace communities in ways a secular ethics council would flag. Alcohol companies are categorically excluded under Sharia regardless of harm-reduction evidence. Secular ethics frameworks, at their best, track consequences. Religious frameworks track commands. Where they agree, the agreement is coincidental; where they diverge, there is no common procedure for resolution.

## Transparency and the secular public

There is a transparency problem that has received almost no attention. When a secular state issues a sukuk, or when a public pension fund co-invests with an Islamic sovereign fund, most citizens have no idea that some of the rules governing their public money were set by religious scholars. This is not necessarily a betrayal of secular principles — legal and financial complexity means that layers of governance always exist — but it is a gap in democratic legibility.

Secular governance rests on the idea that public decisions should be accountable to publics. If a government wanted to issue bonds whose terms were certified by a panel of Catholic bishops, or whose exclusions were determined by a rabbinical court, there would likely be significant political controversy. Islamic sukuk attract almost none, partly because they are genuinely legal, partly because the arrangements are technical and obscure, and partly because the alternative — forgoing large pools of capital — has real costs.

The honest secular response is not to ban these arrangements but to disclose them clearly and to think carefully about where the state's own principles require it to draw limits. A secular government can legitimately issue a sukuk. It should not pretend, in doing so, that the religious governance layer does not exist.

## What secularism actually requires here

Secularism does not require that the state refuse to do business with religious institutions. It requires that the state's own authority remain grounded in non-religious principles, applied equally regardless of faith. By that standard, issuing a sukuk is probably permissible — the state is accessing a capital market, not adopting a theology. Co-investing with a Sharia-compliant fund is permissible — the state does not control the other party's internal governance. What would be impermissible is allowing religious governance to substitute for secular law: letting a Sharia board set the terms of public infrastructure access, for instance, or structuring a public institution so that it formally defers to religious scholars on investment decisions.

The line is genuine but it is not always easy to locate. As Gulf sovereign funds continue to expand and as Islamic finance grows as a share of global capital markets — it is projected to exceed $6 trillion by 2026 — secular states will encounter this problem with increasing frequency. The question of how public money behaves when it touches capital governed by religious law deserves more serious analysis than it currently receives. It is not a crisis, but it is a live issue in applied secularism, one that sits entirely outside the culture-war arguments that usually dominate the subject.

The fact that it is boring and technical is not a reason to ignore it. It may be a reason it has been ignored until now.
