When someone asks why water extinguishes fire, they might want a causal story about heat transfer and oxygen displacement. When someone asks why water is water, they want something different — an account of what water actually is. These are two genuinely distinct kinds of explanation, and conflating them generates some of the most persistent confusions in philosophy, science, and theology alike.

## The basic distinction

A **causal explanation** answers the question "what brought this about?" It traces a sequence of events, forces, or mechanisms that produced the thing in question. Striking a match caused the fire; cortisol release caused the stress response; the meteor impact caused the extinction event. The explanans (the explaining thing) is temporally prior to, or at least distinct from, the explanandum (the thing explained).

A **constitutive explanation** answers a different question: "what is this thing made of, or what makes it the kind of thing it is?" It does not invoke a prior cause but rather specifies the components, relations, or properties that together constitute the phenomenon. Water is constituted by H₂O molecules arranged in a certain way. Pain, on one functionalist account, is constituted by a functional role — being caused by tissue damage and causing avoidance behaviour. The explanans does not precede the explanandum; it *is* the explanandum, described at a different level.

Philosophers sometimes label these the "what" and the "why," but that understates the point. Both answer "why" in ordinary English. The difference is about the direction of the explanatory arrow: causal explanation runs along the temporal axis; constitutive explanation runs across levels of description.

## Why the distinction is easy to miss

Natural language does not flag which kind of explanation is being offered. "Heat makes gases expand" can be read causally (warming a gas produces expansion) or constitutively (expansion just is what increased mean kinetic energy looks like at the macro scale). In practice, both readings are often simultaneously available, which makes it tempting to assume they are always the same.

They are not. Consider pain. One question is what causes pain — nociceptors firing, neural signals reaching the somatosensory cortex. Another question is what pain *is* — whether it is identical to those neural events, constituted by them, or something over and above them. Answering the first question brilliantly does not automatically answer the second. Neuroscience can fully map the causal story of pain without settling whether phenomenal experience is identical to, realised by, or merely correlated with that neural activity. The hard problem of consciousness is hard precisely because the causal story, however complete, seems to leave the constitutive question untouched.

This gap shows up in the debate between **type identity theory** and **functionalism**. Identity theorists say pain is identical to (constituted by) a specific neural state, say C-fibre firing. Functionalists say pain is constituted by a functional role that could be realised in many physical substrates. Both positions are answering the constitutive question. They disagree about what the constituting base is, not about whether there is a causal story. Getting clear on this prevents the mistake of thinking that discovering the neural correlates of consciousness has settled whether consciousness is physical — it hasn't, because correlation is a causal notion, and the constitutive question is whether the correlated items are identical or merely related.

## Grounding and the metaphysics of constitution

In recent analytic metaphysics, the constitutive relation has been formalised under the concept of **grounding**. To say that moral facts are grounded in natural facts, or that mental states are grounded in physical states, is to make a claim about constitutive dependence: the higher-level facts hold *in virtue of* the lower-level facts, not merely *because of* them in a causal sense. The "in virtue of" locution is deliberately non-causal. Grounding does not require temporal priority or energy transfer.

This matters practically. Debates about whether moral realism is compatible with naturalism often stall because participants switch between asking "can natural facts cause us to have moral beliefs?" (causal, epistemological) and "can moral facts be nothing over and above natural facts?" (constitutive, metaphysical). Both questions are live, but they have different implications. One could coherently hold that moral beliefs are caused entirely by natural processes while still maintaining that moral facts are not constitutively reducible to natural facts. Blurring the two questions produces apparent paradoxes that dissolve once the distinction is applied.

Grounding talk has critics. Some philosophers argue that it is obscure — that saying X is grounded in Y, where grounding is neither identity nor causation nor logical entailment, is to invoke a primitive relation we do not understand. This is a fair challenge. The defender of grounding needs to say more about what the relation amounts to. But the challenge is not a reason to collapse constitutive explanation into causal explanation; it is a reason to work harder on the former.

## The theological case

Religious arguments frequently exploit the ambiguity. The cosmological argument in its simplest form asks what caused the universe to exist. The answer proposed — a necessary being, or God — is put forward as a causal explanation. But the argument often slides into constitutive territory: God is not just the first cause but the ground of being, the reason why there is something rather than nothing, the source in whom all things "consist." These are constitutive claims, not causal ones.

The slide matters because the two kinds of explanation face different objections. The causal version faces the regress problem: if everything requires a cause, what caused God? The standard reply — that God is uncaused or self-caused — must be unpacked carefully to avoid incoherence. The constitutive version faces a different problem: what does it mean for contingent things to be "grounded in" a necessary being? Is this a claim about metaphysical dependence, and if so, how is it different from simply asserting that the universe exists necessarily under a different description?

Neither version of the argument is obviously hopeless, but they fail in different ways, and treating them as interchangeable makes it impossible to assess either fairly. A critic who refutes the causal regress argument has not thereby addressed the constitutive version, and vice versa.

## How scientists and philosophers talk past each other

Scientists are professionally trained in causal explanation. Experimental design, statistical inference, and mechanism-seeking are all tools for establishing what causes what. This makes science extraordinarily powerful for a large class of questions. It also creates a professional reflex to translate every question into a causal one.

When philosophers ask constitutive questions — what is life, what is information, what is a species — scientists sometimes hear them as asking for causal histories (how did life originate? how is information transmitted? how do species form?). The causal answers are rich and illuminating. But a complete causal account of how life originated does not settle what life *is*, because two researchers can agree on the entire causal history while disagreeing about whether viruses are alive, whether prions count as living, or where exactly the boundary falls.

This is not a failure of science; it is a different question. Constitutive questions about natural kinds often have partly conventional answers: we draw the boundary of "life" or "species" in ways that serve our explanatory and practical purposes, and the drawing is partly stipulative. Recognising this is not a retreat from scientific rigour; it is rigour applied to the right question.

## What the distinction demands of us

Keeping constitutive and causal explanations distinct is not a pedantic exercise. It determines what counts as an adequate answer to a given question and what work remains once an answer is given.

In ethics, it means recognising that showing how moral beliefs evolved (a causal story) does not by itself show what moral facts are or whether they exist (a constitutive and metaphysical story). The genetic fallacy — dismissing a belief because of its causal origin — is partly a failure to keep these tracks separate. Moral beliefs that evolved for adaptive reasons might still track genuine moral facts; that question requires constitutive analysis, not just evolutionary history.

In philosophy of mind, it means holding open the constitutive question even when the causal neuroscience is complete. It means not assuming that because we can explain every causal transition in a cognitive process, we have explained what experience is.

In theology and cosmology, it means asking, for any proposed explanation of existence, whether the explanation is causal, constitutive, or some mixture — and then pressing on each part separately.

The philosopher Jonathan Schaffer has argued that grounding, not causation, is the fundamental explanatory relation in metaphysics. That strong claim is contested. But the weaker point behind it is secure: causal explanation and constitutive explanation are different tools, they address different questions, and having one does not give you the other. Mixing them up does not just produce bad philosophy; it produces bad arguments that are hard to diagnose precisely because the error is invisible when you are not looking for it.
