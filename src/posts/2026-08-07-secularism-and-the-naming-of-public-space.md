Most debates about secularism focus on what the state does — which prayers it permits, which symbols it displays, which schools it funds. Fewer focus on what the state *says*, specifically the names it gives to the places everyone must share. Yet toponymy — the naming of public space — turns out to be a surprisingly rich test case for what secular neutrality actually requires.

## The landscape as a religious text

Walk through almost any city in the historically Christian West and you are moving through a devotional map. San Francisco, Los Angeles, Santa Fe, São Paulo, Santiago: entire metropolises named for saints. Smaller features multiply the effect — streets called Via Crucis, Angel Grove, Corpus Christi Bay. In Muslim-majority countries the pattern runs differently but just as densely: roads named for companions of the Prophet, squares bearing Quranic epithets, districts whose names encode cosmological claims.

This is not ornamental. Names do conceptual work. They communicate whose history the state considers worth commemorating, which figures belong to the shared civic story. When a government maintains a street called St. Patrick's Avenue or Prophet's Road without ever considering an alternative, it is making a quiet but legible statement about the default religious identity of the polity. The statement is quiet precisely because the names are old; familiarity has bleached out the theology. But bleaching is not the same as neutrality.

## The difference between inheritance and endorsement

A standard secular response to all of this is: inherited names are not endorsements. The French Republic keeps the city of Saint-Étienne on its maps without thereby committing to the intercession of the martyr Stephen. France is among the most rigidly secular states on earth, and it has not renamed a single saint-named commune since the Revolution, apart from a brief Jacobin episode that lasted about a decade before being quietly reversed. If laïcité's champions are relaxed about Saint-Nazaire, perhaps the concern is overblown.

There is something to this. **Historical sediment** is not the same as active promotion. A government that refuses to display a crucifix in a courtroom while maintaining a road called Cathedral Street is not being straightforwardly inconsistent; one act is present speech, the other is the passive maintenance of a historical artifact. The distinction matters morally. We do not require secular states to demolish old churches; we require them not to build new ones with public money.

But the distinction has limits. At some point the passive maintenance of a religious landscape becomes an active choice, because the alternative — renaming — is always available. Every year that a legislature declines to rename Saint John's Park is a year in which it has chosen, consciously or not, to preserve the religious designation. The "mere inheritance" defense weakens as the state's general commitment to religious neutrality strengthens. A state that vigorously enforces neutrality in school prayers while ignoring the theological content of its street signs is applying its principles selectively, and it owes an account of why.

## What actual renaming cases reveal

The cases where renaming has been seriously proposed or carried out illuminate the real pressures at stake. In post-colonial contexts, renaming has been primarily about removing colonial figures rather than religious ones — Salisbury became Harare, Leopoldville became Kinshasa — but the logic is structurally identical: a new political order refuses to let the public landscape continue to speak in the old order's voice.

Closer to the religion question, some French municipalities briefly considered renaming themselves during the fierce laïcité debates of the early 2000s. The idea went nowhere, partly because the administrative disruption is enormous (every address must change, every piece of official correspondence), and partly because the political cost of appearing to attack Christianity outweighed any principled gain. This is honest, and it tells us something: **consistency is not the only value in democratic politics**. Disruption, social cohesion, and the difference between symbolic and material stakes all legitimately enter the calculation.

In Turkey, the post-Ottoman secular state under Atatürk actually did pursue systematic renaming — replacing Arabic and Persian place names with Turkish ones, and stripping many locations of Islamic designations. The project was driven as much by ethnic nationalism as by secularism, and it came with authoritarian tools that liberal secularism cannot and should not use. The Turkish case is a warning that the impulse to secularize public language, taken to its logical extreme and applied by state fiat, can become its own form of cultural coercion — replacing one orthodoxy with another.

## The asymmetry problem

One genuinely difficult issue is asymmetry of scrutiny. In many Western countries, a new street named after a saint would provoke objections that an old one does not. This creates an odd situation: the threshold for religious content in the public landscape depends on when the content arrived, not on what it says. New religious naming faces resistance that old religious naming does not.

From a strict secularist standpoint, this is incoherent. If "St. Mary's Road" is acceptable because it is old, then "St. Mary's Road" would be acceptable if built tomorrow, and no one believes that. The asymmetry is in practice a kind of grandfather clause for Christian (and in some places Islamic or Jewish) heritage, and it reflects the historical dominance of those traditions rather than any principled account of what neutrality permits.

One principled resolution is **procedural**: apply consistent rules going forward while leaving the inherited landscape alone, with the understanding that democratic communities can choose to rename if they wish but are not obligated to. This is not entirely satisfying — it still encodes a preference for whatever religion got there first — but it reflects a real difference between the costs of action and inaction, and it avoids the authoritarian trap of mandating secularization from above.

Another resolution is **functional**: evaluate names not by their origins but by their current effects. If no reasonable citizen of San Francisco experiences the city's name as an imposition of Franciscan theology, then the secular problem, if it ever existed, has dissolved through cultural dilution. Under this view, what matters is whether a name is doing active religious work — whether it functions as advocacy — rather than whether it has theological origins. "Santa Fe" does less theological work today than a newly named "Faith Boulevard" would, even if both reference Christianity.

## What minority communities experience

The functional account has an important challenge: whose experience counts? Majority members of a historically Christian or Muslim society may find saint and prophet names entirely neutral; they have been normalized into inaudibility. But members of religious minorities, or of no religion, may hear them differently. An atheist or a Hindu moving to a new city and finding that every second street invokes a Christian figure may reasonably feel that the public space does not neutrally belong to them.

This is not just a feeling. Research in belonging and civic identity suggests that symbolic environments affect the sense of being a full participant in a political community. **Toponymic exclusion** — the experience of moving through a landscape that implicitly addresses someone else — is a mild but real form of the broader problem of majority cultural defaults. It rarely rises to the level of harm that would justify compulsory state action, but it is a legitimate consideration in debates about how communities choose to name new streets, parks, and buildings.

The practical upshot is modest but clear: secular states should, as a default, name new public spaces after secular figures, natural features, or civic concepts, and should treat the designation of new spaces with religious names as something that requires justification rather than something that passes unremarked. That is not a demand to erase history. It is a demand for consistency between declared principles and forward-looking practice.

## What secularism actually requires here

Nothing about the religious geography of inherited names rises to a constitutional crisis in most democracies. The human stakes are low compared with, say, religious exemptions from anti-discrimination law or state funding of confessional schools. But the question of public naming matters because it is a case where secularism's demands are not enforced by courts or constitutions — where the only discipline is principled self-consistency.

What the analysis suggests is roughly this. Secularism does not require the renaming of historically inherited places; that would be both impractical and, in authoritarian hands, dangerous. It does require that states apply consistent standards to new naming decisions. It does require that the "it's just history" defense be recognized for what it is — a pragmatic accommodation, not a philosophical vindication of the status quo. And it should prompt the acknowledgment, rarely made explicitly, that the public landscape of most states is not religiously neutral: it is religiously sedimented, a record of whose voices shaped the polity before anyone was asking these questions.

A secular state is not one that has no religious history. It is one that takes seriously the obligation to govern for everyone — and that means being honest about where the landscape still speaks in someone else's prayer.
