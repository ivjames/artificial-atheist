The war memorial is among the most emotionally charged objects a secular state produces. It asks citizens to gather, to be silent, to remember the dead — and it does all of this in a register borrowed almost entirely from religion, while insisting it belongs to everyone.

## What makes a memorial feel sacred

The sociologist Émile Durkheim drew a distinction between the sacred and the profane that has nothing to do with God. Sacred things, he argued, are those set apart, treated with special care, approached with altered behaviour. By that measure, war memorials are unambiguously sacred objects. You do not eat lunch on the steps of the Cenotaph. You do not laugh loudly at the Vietnam Veterans Memorial Wall. Visitors lower their voices, slow their pace, and in many cultures bow their heads — gestures learned in places of worship and imported wholesale into civic space.

This borrowing is not accidental. When modern nation-states began constructing large-scale war memorials after 1918, they were dealing with a grief so vast that existing religious infrastructure could not contain it. The Cenotaph in London, designed by Edwin Lutyens and unveiled in 1920, is deliberately stripped of Christian symbolism. Lutyens, himself drawn to theosophy, produced something that reads as sacred without committing to any creed. The result was a monument that nonconformists, Catholics, Jews, and the irreligious could all stand before. The secular state had, perhaps for the first time at this scale, manufactured collective solemnity without theological content.

## The problem that emerges when symbols are added

The neutral template rarely holds. Pressure to add religious content to memorials arrives quickly, usually from the dominant faith community, and it is politically difficult to resist. A cross on a hillside memorial is not experienced by its supporters as an exclusion of non-Christians; it is experienced as the natural language of grief and sacrifice. The legal and civic conflict this produces is not abstract.

In the United States, the **Bladensburg Peace Cross** — a 40-foot Latin cross erected in Maryland in 1925 to commemorate World War I dead — became the subject of a Supreme Court case decided in 2019. The court ruled in *American Legion v. American Humanist Association* that the cross could remain, partly on the grounds that its age had given it a secular historical meaning. The reasoning was contested, but the underlying tension it exposed was real: when a symbol has both religious and commemorative significance, which reading governs?

The answer cannot be purely historical. A Jewish veteran's family, or a Muslim soldier's descendants, standing before a Latin cross that officially honours their relative alongside others, are not wrong to notice that the symbol was not chosen with them in mind. Secular critics of the Bladensburg ruling argued that the "historical meaning" rationale is a device for allowing majority-religion symbols to accumulate squatter's rights in public space. That is a serious point, not a paranoid one.

## When the state performs grief

Beyond fixed monuments, secular states also conduct memorial ceremonies — Remembrance Day parades, national days of mourning, state funerals for military figures. These events raise a different set of questions. A monument can at least be walked past; a ceremony demands participation, or at least presence.

Most such ceremonies in historically Christian democracies include prayers, hymns, or blessings. In the United Kingdom, the annual Remembrance Sunday service at the Cenotaph is structured around Church of England liturgy, led by the Bishop of London. The monarch attends. So do the leaders of all major political parties, the heads of the armed forces, and representatives of foreign governments — regardless of their beliefs. The implicit message is that this is the national form of grief, and to stand outside it is to stand outside the nation.

This is precisely the kind of arrangement that secularist theory aims to interrogate. The philosopher Charles Taylor distinguishes between a **secular state** — one that does not establish a religion — and a **secular society** — one in which religious practice has receded from public life. A state can be formally secular while its commemorative culture remains thoroughly religious. Britain is a sharp example: an established church, a monarch who is its supreme governor, and a national memorial tradition that treats Anglican rites as the default grammar of public sorrow.

The counterargument, made sincerely by defenders of these arrangements, is that the religious content reflects the sincere beliefs of most of those who died, and honouring them in the idiom they would have recognised is itself a form of respect. This has some force. But it runs into a problem of representation: as the religious makeup of armed forces changes — and in most Western countries it has changed substantially — the commemorative idiom either updates or it begins to honour an imagined past rather than the actual dead.

## Secular alternatives and what they actually look like

Several states have developed commemorative forms that deliberately reduce or eliminate religious content. France, whose constitutional **laïcité** is stricter than most, conducts state commemorations without clergy. The ceremonies at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier beneath the Arc de Triomphe involve the flame, the tricolour, silence, and the presence of elected officials — but no liturgy, no prayer, no priest. The sacred atmosphere is achieved through civic symbolism alone.

This model is not without its critics. Some French veterans' associations have complained that stripping religious content makes the ceremonies feel cold, bureaucratic, transactional. That reaction is worth taking seriously as evidence about what the ritual is doing. If the removal of prayer makes the ceremony feel less meaningful, the question is whether that meaning was produced by the content of the belief or simply by the structure of the ritual — the music, the silence, the collective attention. Research on secular ritual suggests the latter can carry most of the weight. What people often miss is not God but the formality, the communal body in shared posture, the sense that ordinary time has been suspended.

New Zealand has, over several decades, expanded its ANZAC Day commemorations to include Māori ceremonial elements alongside Christian ones, and increasingly to acknowledge non-religious participants explicitly. The result is a ceremony that is more complex and occasionally awkward, but arguably more honest about the plural society it represents.

## The political economy of grief

War memorials are not built without money, and money in secular states comes from taxpayers of every belief and none. This creates an accountability question that does not arise for privately funded religious monuments. When a state erects or maintains a memorial with explicitly religious iconography, it is spending the taxes of atheists, of minority-faith citizens, and of those whose relatives the war killed, on symbols that carry a theological claim those relatives may have rejected.

This is the fiscal version of the representation problem, and it maps directly onto debates in other areas of secular policy — the public funding of religious schools, the tax exemption of religious organisations, the use of public land for religious structures. In each case the underlying question is the same: does a pluralist state have a coherent reason to prefer one religious idiom over others, or over none?

The usual answer given by defenders of Christian memorial symbolism is that these are culturally Christian countries, and cultural Christianity is not the same as religious establishment. That distinction is real but fragile. A state can be culturally shaped by a religion without endorsing it institutionally; but when that cultural shaping is expressed through publicly funded monuments and officially conducted ceremonies, the line between cultural inheritance and institutional preference begins to blur.

## What a principled approach looks like

A secular state that takes its own principles seriously would not necessarily strip all religious content from memorials and ceremonies. It might instead apply a test of **proportional inclusion**: does the commemorative practice reflect the actual diversity of those being commemorated, and of those who gather to remember them?

This test permits religious content — it does not mandate blank minimalism — but it requires that such content not function as a default that erases those who do not share it. In practical terms it might mean multi-faith ceremonies that include humanist readings alongside religious ones, monument designs that avoid symbols with denominational specificity, and explicit acknowledgment in official services that the irreligious dead served and died alongside the devout.

None of this is simple to implement, and there are genuine costs: longer ceremonies, design by committee, the loss of the austere simplicity that makes some memorials so powerful. These are real trade-offs, not pretexts. But they are the trade-offs a genuinely secular state must be willing to make if its commemorative culture is to match its constitutional commitments.

The war memorial is a mirror. It shows a society what it thinks grief looks like, who it believes deserves to be remembered, and in whose name the remembering is done. A secular state that has not examined that mirror carefully has not yet finished the work of secularism.
