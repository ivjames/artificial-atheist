Diplomacy is the face a state turns toward the world, and that face is never entirely neutral. When a secular government appoints an ambassador, accredits a consul, or negotiates a treaty with a theocratic counterpart, the separation of church and state that operates domestically runs into pressures it was never designed to handle.

## The domestic model does not export cleanly

Secularism, at its core, is a principle about how a state governs its own territory and citizens. Courts, schools, hospitals, and legislatures can be regulated from the inside. Foreign policy cannot. When France — constitutionally committed to *laïcité* — sends a career diplomat to Riyadh, that diplomat operates inside a legal and social framework where religion is not merely present but sovereign. The French state cannot impose its secular norms on Saudi Arabia, yet it also cannot pretend that its representative is functioning in a neutral environment.

The same asymmetry appears in the other direction. When the Vatican — itself a sovereign state — sends a papal nuncio to a secular democracy, the receiving country faces a choice: treat the nuncio as an ordinary diplomat, or acknowledge that this envoy represents an institution whose authority is explicitly theological. Most secular states choose the former without much reflection, because the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961) is silent on religion. The convention governs function, not faith.

## Whose religious holidays does the embassy observe?

A concrete pressure point emerges in the question of the working calendar. A secular state's embassy in Tel Aviv must decide whether to close on Yom Kippur. An embassy in Islamabad must decide how to handle Ramadan hours, Friday prayers, and Eid al-Fitr. These are not merely logistical questions. Each decision is a small act of either accommodation or resistance.

Most foreign ministries handle this pragmatically: embassies close on the host country's public holidays, which are often religiously defined. This is operationally sensible — staff cannot conduct business if local ministries are closed — but it means that secular governments are, in practice, organising their overseas operations around religious calendars they have officially disavowed at home. The French foreign ministry does not recognise Christmas as a state holiday domestically in any religiously significant sense, yet French embassies observe local religious public holidays as a matter of course.

This is not hypocrisy so much as an unexamined gap between principle and practice. The gap matters because it illustrates that **procedural secularism** — the idea that the state remains neutral on religious truth claims — is far easier to maintain within a single jurisdiction than across borders where the host state has made different choices.

## Religious literacy as a diplomatic competency

A harder question is whether diplomats serving in religiously significant postings should have formal training in the religion of the host country, and if so, what that training is for. The United States Foreign Service Institute offers language training, area studies, and cross-cultural communication. Religious content is present but not systematically required. The United Kingdom's Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office has, since around 2018, pushed for greater **religious literacy** among its diplomats, partly in response to criticism that secular-minded officials consistently misread religiously motivated actors — from Iranian clerics to Nigerian Pentecostalists — as behaving irrationally when in fact they are behaving according to coherent but unfamiliar frameworks.

The argument for religious literacy is not that diplomats should become believers or pretend sympathy they do not have. It is that effective secular diplomacy requires understanding religion as a real social force, not as a residue that modernisation will eventually dissolve. A diplomat who cannot read the difference between a fatwa issued for political cover and one issued from genuine doctrinal conviction is poorly equipped for the Middle East, South Asia, or sub-Saharan Africa.

The counterargument, worth taking seriously, is that mandating religious literacy risks encoding a particular view of religion — as always-sincere, always-community-forming, always to be accommodated — that is itself a kind of theological assumption. Secular governments might reasonably insist that religion is one variable among many rather than a special category requiring special hermeneutics. Both positions reflect genuine choices about what secularism means in practice.

## Treaty obligations and religious states

When a secular democracy signs a bilateral investment treaty, a trade agreement, or a legal assistance pact with a state governed by religious law, the documents must somehow bridge two different legitimating frameworks. Saudi Arabia's commercial law is grounded partly in Sharia; prohibitions on interest (*riba*) affect how contracts can be structured. Iran's civil code draws on Shia jurisprudence in ways that affect inheritance, testimony, and family law — all of which can become relevant in cross-border disputes.

Secular states handle this mostly through private international law: each jurisdiction applies its own conflict-of-laws rules to determine which country's law governs a given dispute. But this technical solution obscures a normative question that secular governments rarely confront directly: is there a principled limit to what a secular state should agree to enforce on behalf of a religious legal system? If a Saudi court awards an inheritance using Sharia's differential treatment of male and female heirs, should a French court enforce that judgment? French private international law has a **public policy exception** (*ordre public*) that can refuse enforcement of foreign judgments that violate fundamental French values. Courts have used this exception to block enforcement of Sharia-based inheritance distributions. But the exception is invoked inconsistently and often after the fact.

The principled secular position would be to state clearly, in advance, which categories of foreign religious law a secular state will not enforce and why — on the grounds that certain rights (equal inheritance, testimony without religious qualification) are not negotiable even in cross-border contexts. Few governments have done this with any rigour.

## The ambassador as symbolic actor

Beyond law and logistics, there is the question of what ambassadors do ceremonially. Ambassadors attend state funerals, national day receptions, and inaugurations, most of which — in religious states — involve explicit religious ritual. An ambassador from a secular republic who bows his head during a royal prayer, or who attends a mosque opening as part of official duties, is participating in religious symbolism whether he intends to or not.

Some secular states have developed informal guidance: participate in the ceremony, but as an observer rather than a worshipper; do not genuflect; do not recite prayers. But the line between respectful attendance and symbolic endorsement is genuinely unclear, and different ambassadors draw it differently. When Israel's president attends an official ceremony in a secular European capital and a local religious figure leads the opening prayer, European heads of protocol must decide whether to include a prayer at all, how long it should be, and whether other religious traditions should be represented. The same decisions multiply when the venue is abroad and the host is a theocracy.

What this reveals is that **diplomatic ceremony is a form of implicit speech**, and secular states have not thought carefully about what they are saying when their representatives participate in it.

## When the posting is the Vatican

No diplomatic posting crystallises these tensions more sharply than the question of whether secular states should maintain full ambassadorial relations with the Holy See. Currently, over 180 states do. The Holy See is recognised under international law as a sovereign entity, distinct from Vatican City, and its diplomatic status predates modern international law. Its nuncios carry the automatic rank of dean of the diplomatic corps in Catholic countries — a privilege few secular states have formally challenged.

Critics of this arrangement argue that secular states have no principled reason to treat a religious institution as a sovereign state, and that doing so gives the Catholic Church a formal diplomatic voice in international forums — including the United Nations, where the Holy See holds permanent observer status — that no other religious institution enjoys. The Church has used this status to lobby against reproductive rights provisions in UN documents, to oppose certain formulations of gender equality, and to resist provisions on sexual and reproductive health. Whether one agrees with those positions or not, the question of whether a secular state's tax-funded diplomatic apparatus should facilitate them is a genuine one.

The standard defence is pragmatic: the Holy See has real global influence, runs the largest non-governmental network of schools and hospitals in the world, and is a useful back-channel in conflicts from Colombia to the Philippines. These are reasonable arguments. But they are arguments for strategic engagement, not for treating the Vatican's sovereignty claim as unproblematic. A secular government can engage with a religious institution without granting it the diplomatic standing of a state — and the failure to distinguish between these two things reflects, once again, a gap between secular principle and secular practice.

## What rigorous secularism would require

None of this suggests that secular states should become diplomatically isolationist or refuse to deal with religious counterparts. The world contains religious states, and ignoring them is not an option. What rigorous secularism in foreign policy would require is something more modest: consistency, transparency, and articulacy about the choices being made.

A secular government that closes its embassies on religious holidays abroad should acknowledge that it is making an accommodation and explain its reasoning. A foreign ministry that trains diplomats in religious literacy should be clear that the purpose is analytical, not devotional. A government that signs a treaty with a state whose legal system is religiously grounded should state, in advance, which provisions of that system it will and will not enforce. A state that maintains full diplomatic relations with the Holy See should be able to articulate why it treats that entity differently from, say, the World Council of Churches or the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation.

The domestic architecture of secularism — separation of powers, church-state boundary, neutrality on religious truth — was built to solve problems inside a single jurisdiction. Exporting that architecture, or protecting it when it runs up against incompatible systems abroad, requires exactly the kind of principled reasoning that secular governance claims to value. The diplomatic context is one place where that reasoning has, so far, mostly been left undone.
