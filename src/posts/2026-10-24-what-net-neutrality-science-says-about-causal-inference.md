Causal claims are everywhere in science. A drug lowers blood pressure. Smoking causes lung cancer. Lead exposure reduces IQ. These statements feel straightforward, but establishing that one thing genuinely *causes* another — rather than merely correlates with it — is one of the hardest problems in empirical research.

## Why correlation falls short

The basic problem is selection. When we observe that people who eat more vegetables have fewer heart attacks, we cannot immediately conclude that vegetables protect the heart. People who eat vegetables also tend to exercise more, smoke less, and have higher incomes. Any of these factors, or some unobserved combination of them, might be doing the causal work.

This is the **confounding problem**: a third variable influences both the exposure and the outcome, producing an association that mimics causation. It is not a minor technical nuisance. Confounding has derailed major research programs. For decades, observational evidence suggested that hormone replacement therapy protected post-menopausal women against heart disease. When the Women's Health Initiative ran a proper randomised trial, it found the opposite. The observational signal had been driven by the fact that women who chose HRT tended to be healthier and wealthier to begin with — the **healthy user bias**.

The randomised controlled trial solves this cleanly in principle. By randomly assigning participants to treatment or control groups, randomisation distributes both observed and unobserved confounders evenly across groups. Any difference in outcomes can then be attributed to the treatment. This is why the RCT is called the gold standard. The logic is sound.

## What we do when randomisation is impossible

The difficulty is that most scientifically interesting causal questions cannot be answered by running an RCT. We cannot randomly assign people to smoke for thirty years, to be born into poverty, or to live near a chemical plant. We cannot randomise countries to adopt different tax policies or randomly assign historical events to test counterfactuals. Epidemiology, economics, political science, and much of psychology must work with data that were generated by the world rather than by a researcher's coin flip.

This has driven the development of a toolkit of **quasi-experimental methods** — designs that exploit naturally occurring sources of variation to approximate the conditions of a randomised experiment.

**Instrumental variable (IV) analysis** is one such tool. The idea is to find a variable — the instrument — that affects the exposure but has no direct effect on the outcome except through the exposure. A classic example is using the distance from a patient's home to a specialist hospital as an instrument for receiving specialist care. Distance plausibly affects whether someone receives care but does not directly affect their health outcome. The variation in care driven by distance is therefore as-good-as-random, and a researcher can use it to isolate the causal effect of specialist treatment. IV analysis is powerful but demanding: the instrument must be genuinely unrelated to the outcome through any path other than the exposure, and this assumption is often untestable.

**Regression discontinuity design** exploits sharp thresholds. Imagine a scholarship program that awards funding to students who score above 70 on an entrance exam. Students who score 69 and 71 are nearly identical in ability, but one group gets the scholarship and the other does not. By comparing outcomes for students just below and just above the cutoff, researchers can estimate the causal effect of the scholarship, because assignment near the threshold is effectively random. This design has been used to study the effects of educational interventions, minimum wage laws, and medical eligibility criteria.

**Difference-in-differences (DiD)** compares changes over time between a group that was exposed to some policy or event and a control group that was not. If a new minimum wage law passes in one state but not in neighbouring states, researchers can compare how employment changed in the treated state relative to the control states. The logic rests on a **parallel trends assumption**: absent the intervention, the two groups would have followed the same trajectory. This assumption cannot be proven, only made more or less plausible.

## The problem with assumptions

Every quasi-experimental method rests on assumptions that cannot be fully verified. This is the central tension in causal inference outside the RCT. The assumptions are not arbitrary — they are grounded in theory and prior knowledge, and their plausibility can be partially tested. But they are never guaranteed.

This matters for how we interpret the results. A coefficient from an IV analysis is not a fact about the world in the same unambiguous way that a randomised experiment's result is — it is a fact about the world *conditional on the instrument being valid*. If that assumption fails even partially, the estimate can be wrong in ways that are difficult to detect.

Researchers have developed tools to probe these assumptions. **Sensitivity analyses** ask: how strong would an unmeasured confounder need to be to overturn this result? **Placebo tests** check whether the design produces spurious effects where none should exist. Multiple instruments or multiple quasi-experimental designs pointing to the same answer increase confidence. But none of this eliminates uncertainty — it characterises it.

This is not a counsel of despair. It is a call for calibrated confidence. The causal inference toolkit, used carefully and transparently, has produced genuine knowledge. The link between smoking and lung cancer was established largely through observational methods before animal experiments and molecular biology confirmed the mechanism. The evidence on leaded petrol and cognitive development accumulated through a combination of natural experiments, blood-lead data, and cross-country comparisons long before randomised evidence was conceivable.

## Directed acyclic graphs and the new causal revolution

A major development in causal inference over the past three decades has been the formalisation of causal reasoning through **directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)**, associated with the work of Judea Pearl and colleagues. A DAG is a diagram in which nodes represent variables and arrows represent direct causal relationships. The structure of the graph encodes assumptions about what causes what.

DAGs are useful for several reasons. They make assumptions explicit and visual. They identify which variables a researcher needs to control for — and which variables they must *not* control for. This second point is counterintuitive. Statisticians have long been taught to control for more variables to reduce confounding, but DAGs show that controlling for certain variables can actually introduce bias. **Collider bias** occurs when a researcher conditions on a variable that is caused by both the exposure and the outcome. Doing so opens a spurious pathway between them. For example, if both smoking and a genetic variant cause a particular disease, and a study selects only people with that disease, controlling for disease status creates a spurious inverse association between smoking and the genetic variant. This is not merely theoretical — collider bias is now recognised as a likely source of error in dozens of published studies.

The DAG framework also clarifies what it means to estimate a causal effect at all. The **potential outcomes framework**, developed separately by Donald Rubin, defines the causal effect of a treatment as the difference between what would have happened to a unit if treated versus if untreated. Only one of these outcomes is ever observed — the other is a **counterfactual**. Causal inference, on this view, is fundamentally about the estimation of counterfactual states of the world that we can never directly see.

## What this means for scientific communication

The gap between what causal inference methods establish and how results are communicated is a serious problem. Observational studies that identify correlations are routinely reported — by researchers, institutions, and journalists — as if they demonstrate causation. "Coffee linked to lower risk of Alzheimer's" becomes "Coffee protects the brain." The hedging that should accompany a correlation is stripped away.

This is not just sloppy writing. It shapes public understanding and policy in ways that matter. When the causal claims later fail to replicate, or when a proper trial finds a null result, trust in science erodes. The damage is compounded because the failure often looks like science contradicting itself rather than science correcting a mistaken inferential leap.

Better scientific communication requires making causal claims and correlational claims look different. Journals and press offices can take partial responsibility here, but readers also benefit from knowing the distinction. An association, even a large and consistent one, does not establish causation until the confounding pathways have been credibly closed. The strength of an association, its consistency across studies, its biological plausibility, and the presence of a dose-response relationship all raise or lower the probability that the relationship is causal — this was Bradford Hill's insight in 1965 — but they do not replace the need for careful design.

## Why this matters beyond methodology

The problem of causal inference is not merely technical. It connects directly to questions about what science can and cannot tell us about the world, and about how much epistemic humility is warranted when we act on scientific results.

Policy decisions regularly rest on causal claims. Does this educational intervention improve outcomes? Does this drug reduce mortality? Does this regulation change behaviour? When the underlying causal evidence is weak — when it rests on a single observational study with unexamined confounders — acting on it confidently can do harm. When it is strong — when multiple independent methods, each with different potential failure modes, all converge on the same answer — confidence is warranted even without a randomised trial.

The most important lesson from modern causal inference is that the question "does X cause Y?" is not settled by the presence of a correlation, but it is also not unanswerable without an RCT. Between raw association and experimental proof lies a rich and rigorous set of tools, each with named assumptions, testable predictions, and known failure modes. Science operates honestly when it uses them carefully and reports what they actually show.
