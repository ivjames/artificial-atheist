Catholicism has the Magisterium. Islam has chains of scholarly transmission. But what keeps a religion coherent when there is no single body empowered to say what the faith actually requires? The question is less exotic than it sounds — most of the world's believers belong to traditions that have no pope.

## The problem of doctrinal drift

Without a centralising authority, every congregation, teacher, or individual believer is, in principle, free to interpret foundational texts as they see fit. In practice, this produces not chaos but a recognisable spectrum of positions, with some views clustering at the centre and others pushed to the margins. The interesting question is what does the clustering — what substitutes for an official body in producing something that functions like orthodoxy.

One answer is **canon**. A stable set of texts, even without an authoritative interpreter, exerts gravitational force. Texts get memorised, quoted, commented upon, and the weight of prior commentary becomes normative. In Rabbinic Judaism, the Talmud is not the word of a single authority but the record of accumulated dispute; yet certain rulings in it became binding not because a council decreed them but because generations of practice treated them that way. The canon anchors the community even when the community disagrees about what the canon means.

A second answer is **social enforcement**. Communities that share worship, marriage, diet, and education develop strong informal mechanisms for marking deviance. Someone who denies a sufficiently central claim — resurrection in Christianity, the prophethood of Muhammad in Sunni Islam, the authority of the Vedas in classical Hinduism — risks social costs that have nothing to do with any formal tribunal. The boundary between acceptable heterodoxy and unacceptable heresy is drawn not by decree but by accumulated social pressure. This process is slower and messier than a papal ruling, but it is not therefore weaker.

## How Islam manages without a church

The absence of ordained clergy in Sunni Islam is sometimes cited as evidence of radical egalitarianism: any Muslim can lead prayer, any believer can read the sources. The reality is more hierarchical than that framing suggests. The **ulama** — trained scholars — have historically controlled the institutions of legal opinion, and access to those institutions required decades of study in recognised centres of learning. Al-Azhar in Cairo, for instance, has never had legislative power over global Islam, but its prestige has made its rulings influential across the Sunni world for centuries.

What emerges is not a church but a **credentialing ecosystem**. Authority flows from demonstrated mastery of a recognised curriculum, from endorsement by recognised masters, and from the social networks those masters control. Deviations from the mainstream are policed not by excommunication but by the withdrawal of scholarly recognition — a fatwa declared by someone outside the credentialed network carries little weight. The system is decentralised, but it is not flat.

This matters for the question of orthodoxy because it shows that doctrinal consensus can form through reputational mechanisms rather than institutional ones. The consensus may be slower to form and harder to revise, but it is recognisably a consensus. When Ibn Taymiyya challenged the legality of visiting saints' shrines in the fourteenth century, he was imprisoned by political authorities partly at the urging of scholars who found his views disruptive. The mechanism was social and political, not ecclesiastical, but the outcome — the temporary suppression of a minority position — resembled what a church council might have produced.

## Protestant fragmentation and the limits of sola scriptura

The Reformation is the obvious test case for what happens when central authority is explicitly rejected. Luther's insistence that scripture alone — **sola scriptura** — should govern Christian belief was meant to liberate interpretation from papal distortion. Within decades it had produced dozens of mutually incompatible readings of the same texts, and within centuries it had produced tens of thousands of denominations.

This fragmentation is real, but it is worth being precise about what it shows. It does not show that orthodoxy disappears. Within any given Protestant tradition, orthodoxy is often enforced with considerable rigour. A Baptist congregation that began teaching baptismal regeneration would face significant pressure from its denominational network. A Lutheran synod that denied the real presence in the Eucharist would split. The difference from Catholicism is not that Protestants lack orthodoxies but that they have **many competing orthodoxies** with no adjudication mechanism between them.

The result is an interesting epistemic situation. Each tradition claims that its reading of scripture is correct, that the others have erred, and that no external body is competent to settle the dispute. This is not relativism — each tradition typically believes its own orthodoxy is objectively true — but it produces something that functions like relativism from the outside. The absence of a shared adjudicating authority means that inter-denominational disagreements cannot be resolved by appeal to any agreed procedure. They can only be resolved by one side persuading the other, or by the two sides ceasing to care about each other's views.

## Orthodoxy as a historical deposit

One underappreciated mechanism for producing orthodoxy without authority is **temporal momentum**. Beliefs and practices that have been held for a long time accrue a kind of inertial legitimacy that is distinct from any argument for their truth. In many traditions, the antiquity of a practice is itself treated as evidence that it is correct — the **appeal to tradition** is not a fallacy within a framework that holds revelation to be transmitted through history.

This creates a structural conservatism that can be quite powerful. Eastern Orthodoxy, which also lacks a pope (the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople has a primacy of honour, not jurisdiction), has maintained a remarkable degree of liturgical and doctrinal consistency across fifteen centuries and dozens of autocephalous national churches. The mechanism is precisely this depositional logic: what was believed by the Fathers is presumed to be what was revealed. Innovation bears the burden of proof.

The conservatism is not absolute — Orthodoxy has had its own controversies, its iconoclast crisis being the most dramatic — but the bar for doctrinal revision is extremely high precisely because the tradition has no mechanism for authoritative updating. The absence of a council with binding legislative power means that any change requires something close to universal consensus, which makes change rare but also makes what has survived seem settled.

## The sociological view and its limits

Sociologists of religion, following Émile Durkheim, have tended to explain religious orthodoxy as a function of **collective identity**. Doctrinal boundaries, on this view, are primarily markers of group membership rather than sincere metaphysical commitments. What counts as heresy is whatever threatens group cohesion, and what counts as orthodoxy is whatever most members already believe.

This explanation has genuine predictive power. Groups under external pressure tend to enforce orthodoxy more strictly — the boundary-marking function becomes more urgent when the group feels threatened. Minority religious communities in hostile environments often display higher doctrinal conformity than majority communities in tolerant ones. The sociological account explains this pattern well.

But the account is incomplete because it brackets the question of whether any of it is true. Believers generally do not experience orthodoxy as a social technology; they experience it as fidelity to what is real. The sociological lens, taken alone, cannot explain why some doctrinal innovations succeed in displacing old orthodoxies — as happened when Christian abolitionism eventually reshaped mainstream Protestant readings of the Bible — while others fail. Social forces constrain belief, but they do not fully determine its content. The arguments people make to each other about what the texts mean, and whether those arguments are good ones, also matter.

## What this tells the skeptic

The formation of orthodoxy without a centralising authority is a genuinely impressive social achievement. It demonstrates that doctrinal coherence does not require top-down enforcement; it can emerge from canon, credentialing, social pressure, and temporal momentum operating together. For the skeptic, this is worth understanding rather than dismissing.

It is also, however, a reminder that the mechanisms producing orthodoxy are entirely human. They select for social viability, not for truth. A belief can become orthodox because it is ancient, because influential teachers endorsed it, because it coheres with other settled commitments, or because dissent from it was too socially costly — none of which are reliable guides to whether the belief accurately describes the world. The process of orthodoxy formation is explicable without reference to divine guidance, which does not prove divine guidance is absent, but does mean its presence is not required by the evidence.

What decentralised orthodoxy also illustrates is that the diversity of religious belief — across traditions and within them — is not merely a surface feature produced by cultural accident. It is the predictable output of applying interpretive freedom to ambiguous texts in the absence of a shared adjudicating procedure. The disagreements are genuine, and most of the traditions involved cannot all be right. That fact does not need to be hammered; it is simply what follows from taking the traditions' own truth claims seriously.
