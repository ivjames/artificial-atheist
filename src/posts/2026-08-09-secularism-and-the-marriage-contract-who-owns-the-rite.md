Marriage sits at the intersection of the most personal human commitments and the most formal state machinery. Secular democracies have spent two centuries trying to sort out who owns it — and the question remains genuinely unresolved in ways that matter for ordinary people's lives.

## Why marriage became a secular battleground

Marriage was, for most of recorded Western history, a religious institution that states borrowed. Medieval canon law governed who could marry, on what grounds, and with what consequences. Civil registration arrived late: France mandated civil marriage in 1792, England not until 1837, and many countries later still. The logic of that shift was straightforward — if citizens owe legal duties to each other and to the state arising from marriage, the state needs reliable records and consistent rules that do not vary by denomination.

But secularising the paperwork did not secularise the meaning. Most people who marry in a civil registry office still hold a subsequent religious ceremony, and in many jurisdictions the religious ceremony retains full legal force. The UK allows Church of England weddings to be simultaneously civil registrations; Jewish and Quaker ceremonies have enjoyed the same status since 1753. This dual-track arrangement has a surface elegance: the state gets its record, the community gets its rite, nobody is forced to choose. The problem is that it stitches two distinct normative frameworks together without ever clarifying which governs when they conflict.

## The parallel-jurisdiction trap

The clearest evidence of unresolved conflict comes from communities that operate **parallel family law**. In England and Wales, Sharia councils and the Jewish Beth Din both handle religious divorce. Neither body has statutory power — their rulings are not legally binding — but they exercise enormous social authority within their communities. A Muslim woman whose civil marriage has been dissolved may still be considered married under Islamic law until her husband grants a *talaq*, and a Jewish woman may be in the same position without a *get*. The social and sometimes economic consequences of this status are real: remarriage within the community is blocked, children's religious status may be affected, and the leverage this gives to a recalcitrant spouse has been documented repeatedly.

This is not a hypothetical edge case. The charity Agunah Campaign in the UK has tracked cases stretching over years and sometimes decades. The secular state's response has so far been cautious. The Divorce (Financial Provision) Act route — allowing courts to consider the withholding of a religious divorce when making financial settlements — nudges parties without coercing them. Canada's province of Quebec went further in 2021, amending its Civil Code to allow courts to draw adverse inferences and adjust property division when one spouse withholds religious consent. These are pragmatic workarounds, not principled settlements.

The principled question is whether a secular state should recognise religious marriage law at all, or should insist that civil marriage is the only marriage the law knows. The latter position has a clean logic: you may marry in whatever ceremony your faith requires, but legally you are either civilly married or you are not, and the legal consequences attach only to civil status. Several countries, including France and Germany, already require that civil marriage precede or accompany any religious ceremony for the religious rite to have social legitimacy (though not legal compulsion). But even here the parallel structures re-emerge informally, because no secular state can prevent communities from treating religious marriage as more binding than civil marriage in their own social world.

## What recognition actually means

A related problem concerns the scope of **what the state endorses when it recognises religious marriage**. In most common-law jurisdictions, recognising a religious marriage means accepting the ceremony as legally constitutive of a civil status. But religious marriage ceremonies often carry doctrinal content that the state would not otherwise endorse — about the hierarchy of spouses, about indissolubility, about the role of community consent. The state recognises the outcome (a valid marriage) without endorsing the content. This is the standard liberal move: neutrality about comprehensive doctrines, recognition of voluntary associations.

The difficulty is that the content is not always separable from the legal outcome. If a religious authority refuses to solemnise a marriage between two people of the same sex, and the state grants that authority legal recognition for its ceremonies, the state's recognition of the authority is structurally intertwined with the authority's exclusionary practice. The UK's current position — that civil marriage is available to all, but that religious bodies have an opt-in on same-sex ceremonies — attempts to thread this needle. The Church of England is legally prohibited from solemnising same-sex marriages, a prohibition the church itself supported in 2013 as a protection rather than a constraint. This produces the paradox of a liberal state enshrining a religious body's discriminatory practice in statute, at the body's own request, as a concession to institutional autonomy.

## The spousal agreement problem

**Prenuptial and postnuptial agreements** add another layer. A growing number of religious communities produce their own contractual instruments: the Islamic *mahr* (dower), the Jewish *ketubah*, Christian covenant marriage contracts in several US states. Courts in England have moved toward enforcing prenuptial agreements since the 2010 Supreme Court case *Radmacher v Granatino*, and in principle a *mahr* or *ketubah* can be enforced as a contractual term. But this requires the secular court to interpret documents written in religious legal frameworks, sometimes in a different language, sometimes referencing obligations that have no civil-law equivalent.

The result is inconsistency. Courts have enforced *mahr* clauses as straightforward debt obligations, dismissed them as contrary to public policy, and deferred them as unquantifiable — sometimes in cases with materially similar facts. This inconsistency is not judicial failure; it reflects genuine ambiguity about whether secular contract law is the right tool for instruments embedded in a different normative tradition. The question of whether the state should develop a coherent doctrine here, or leave it to case-by-case pragmatism, is one most legislatures have preferred not to confront.

## What secularism actually demands of marriage law

None of the above implies that secular states should strip religious communities of any role in marriage. Freedom of religion includes the freedom to organise communal life around shared practices, including marriage rites that carry meaning the civil registration form never could. A thoroughgoing secularism is not hostile to these practices; it is indifferent to their theological content while remaining committed to protecting the legal rights of the individuals within them.

What this does demand is clarity about the boundary. The principle should be: **civil law governs legal status and all rights that flow from it; religious law governs the meaning a community assigns to that status, without legal force over non-consenting individuals.** Applied consistently, this would mean that no religious divorce tribunal could affect legal rights, that courts need not interpret religious contracts as religious documents (only as civil contracts with particular terms), and that state recognition of religious marriage ceremonies carries no endorsement of the theology those ceremonies embody.

This is more or less the French model, and it is not without costs. It does require people who care about religious solemnisation to perform two ceremonies, or to accept that the civil ceremony is the real one in law. It may feel bureaucratically cold. But it is at least honest about what the state can and cannot do: the state can guarantee equality of legal status; it cannot guarantee that a community will treat a civil divorce as religiously valid, and it should not pretend otherwise.

## The real dispute under the surface

Arguments about marriage law in secular democracies often present themselves as being about practical arrangements — how many ceremonies, whose paperwork, which courts. The real dispute is about something deeper: whether religious communities have a legitimate claim to co-govern family life alongside the state, or whether the state's jurisdiction over the legal incidents of family life is, in democratic principle, exclusive.

Defenders of the first view argue that marriage is pre-political, that communities formed around shared visions of the good have a prior claim on how they structure their most intimate bonds, and that state intrusion into that space is a kind of cultural imperialism dressed up as neutrality. This is not a trivial argument. The history of colonial states using civil marriage law to undermine indigenous family structures is real and well-documented.

Defenders of the second view argue that co-governance produces exactly the parallel-jurisdiction trap described above, that it predictably disadvantages less powerful parties within religious communities (typically women and sexual minorities), and that calling state protection of individual rights "intrusion" confuses institutional autonomy with individual freedom. A religious community's autonomy ends where its members' civil rights begin — and marriage, with its consequences for property, custody, inheritance, and immigration status, is saturated with civil rights.

The secular case does not require dismissing the first view as bad faith. It requires showing that, at the level of legal status and its consequences, the second view better protects the people who are most exposed when the two frameworks collide. The woman waiting years for a *get* her husband will not grant, or the same-sex couple whose community refuses to recognise their civil marriage, are the test cases. A secularism that cannot give them a clear answer has not yet finished the work that civil marriage registration was supposed to begin in 1792.
