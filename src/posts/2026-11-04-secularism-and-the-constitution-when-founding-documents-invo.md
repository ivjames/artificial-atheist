The founding documents of modern states are rarely clean philosophical statements. They are compromises, historical accidents, and rhetorical performances. Some of the most consequential secular democracies in the world open their constitutions with explicit reference to God — and that tension between sacred preamble and secular function has never been fully resolved.

## What religious language in constitutions actually does

The Irish constitution opens by invoking "the Most Holy Trinity" and acknowledging "all our obligations to Our Divine Lord, Jesus Christ." The German Basic Law appeals to "responsibility before God and man." The constitution of India is resolutely secular in its operative articles, yet its preamble was amended in 1976 to describe India as a "secular socialist" republic — wording that has been contested in court ever since. The United States constitution contains no reference to God, but the Declaration of Independence does, and American political culture has treated the two as a package.

Constitutional preambles are not normally justiciable. Courts in most jurisdictions treat them as interpretive aids rather than operative law. But that underestimates their influence. When judges construe ambiguous provisions, preambles set the tone. When legislators debate contested social questions — abortion, marriage, end-of-life law — a preamble invoking God gives religious framings a kind of official warrant. The language is decorative until someone weaponises it, and someone usually does.

## The difference between invocation and establishment

A useful distinction separates **invocatory language** from **establishmentarian structure**. Many constitutions that mention God do not, in their operative articles, give any church legal authority, public funding priority, or doctrinal control over law. Ireland's constitution, for all its Trinitarian opening, also guarantees freedom of conscience and prohibits the state endowment of any religion. Germany's Basic Law coexists with a church tax system but maintains formal separation of ecclesiastical and civil authority.

This distinction matters because critics of religious constitutional language sometimes treat invocation as establishment, and defenders of such language sometimes treat invocation as harmless symbolism. Neither is quite right. Invocatory language is not legally inert, but neither is it equivalent to a state religion. The honest position is that it creates a **soft presumption** — a rhetorical default that shapes political culture without directly commanding any outcome. Soft presumptions are harder to argue against than hard rules, which is precisely why they persist.

## When courts have to interpret divine mandates

The clearest cases of constitutional religion creating real legal problems arise when operative articles — not just preambles — embed theological content. Article 41 of the Irish constitution recognised the "special position" of the Catholic Church until 1972, when it was removed by referendum. Article 44 still guarantees freedom of religion but its original drafting was shaped by Catholic social teaching on the family, producing constitutional constraints on divorce and contraception that required further referendums to dismantle.

Pakistan's constitution declares Islam the state religion and requires that all laws conform to Quranic injunctions. The Federal Shariat Court has jurisdiction to strike down legislation deemed un-Islamic. This is a different beast from a God-invoking preamble — it is explicit theological jurisdiction embedded in positive law. But the trajectory from "our laws reflect divine values" to "our courts enforce divine law" is shorter than secular constitutionalists sometimes acknowledge. The preamble sets a destination; the operative articles determine how far along the road a state has travelled.

India's Supreme Court has repeatedly held that "secularism" as written into the preamble is a **basic structure** of the constitution — meaning no parliament can remove it even by supermajority. That is an unusual move: a court treating a word added by amendment as so fundamental that it now binds all future amendments. Critics argue this freezes constitutional meaning in ways that democratic majorities cannot revisit. Defenders argue it protects minority rights against majoritarian pressure. Both concerns are legitimate, and neither resolves the underlying question of whether courts should be in the business of enforcing a particular constitutional theology about religion's proper place.

## The amendment problem

Constitutional amendment is the standard democratic mechanism for updating founding documents. But religious language in constitutions tends to be peculiarly resistant to amendment for two reasons. First, it is often located in preambles that are not obviously operative, so there is less immediate pressure to change it — it causes harm diffusely rather than directly. Second, any proposal to remove God from a founding document is politically legible as an attack on religion itself, which makes it a losing proposition in almost any democratic context, regardless of the actual legal stakes.

This creates an asymmetry. Religious language enters constitutions during founding moments — often moments of heightened national sentiment where religious identity and civic identity are fused — and then proves nearly impossible to dislodge. The Irish preamble has survived every referendum on specific Catholic social provisions. The U.S. motto "In God We Trust," adopted in 1956 during Cold War anxiety, has survived every constitutional challenge. What enters under conditions of religious majority consensus tends to stay even when that consensus erodes, because the coalition required to remove it is structurally harder to build than the coalition that opposes removal.

## What secular constitutionalism actually requires

Secular constitutionalism does not require that founding documents be theologically sterile. The stronger claim — that any mention of God compromises secular governance — proves too much. A state can acknowledge the religious history of its people without subordinating civil law to religious authority. What secular constitutionalism does require is that the operative machinery of government — legislation, adjudication, administration — function without privileging religious over non-religious reasons, and without treating adherence to any faith as a condition of full citizenship.

By that standard, the problem with religious constitutional language is not its existence but its **downstream effects**. If a God-invoking preamble is used to argue that secular legislation violates the spirit of the constitution, or that non-religious citizens occupy a secondary civic status, then the language has ceased to be decorative. The test is functional: does the religious framing constrain secular law-making in practice? In Ireland between 1937 and the 1990s, it clearly did. In Germany today, it largely does not. The same words carry different weights in different political cultures.

## The case for honest acknowledgment over selective amnesia

There is a temptation, in secular liberal constitutional theory, to simply ignore the religious language in founding documents and get on with the secular work. Courts in many jurisdictions have largely done this, treating Trinitarian preambles as historical curiosities and enforcing secular operative articles without much theological fuss. This is pragmatically effective but intellectually incomplete.

The honest secular position is to acknowledge that many democratic constitutions carry religious freight they have not fully worked through, and that this matters. It matters for citizens who are not religious and who encounter, in the founding document of their state, a declaration that their community owes allegiance to a God they do not recognise. It matters for legal interpretation, because language that is never examined never loses its power to shape judgment in subtle ways. And it matters for democratic legitimacy, because a founding document that reflects the values of one religious tradition — however historically dominant — does not speak equally for everyone.

Acknowledging this does not require constitutional revolution. It requires political honesty about what founding documents actually say, what they historically meant, and what ongoing work remains to ensure that secular governance is not merely tolerated within a theistic constitutional framework but genuinely grounded in one that belongs to all citizens equally.
