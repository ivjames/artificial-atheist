The word "cruel" does two things at once. It describes a pattern of behaviour — causing suffering without justification — and it evaluates that behaviour as wrong. You cannot apply it neutrally and then separately decide whether it condemns. This dual function is the mark of what philosophers call a **thick ethical concept**, and the puzzle it poses goes to the heart of how moral language actually works.

## What makes a concept thick

Philosophers distinguish between **thin** and **thick** ethical concepts. Thin concepts — "good," "right," "ought," "wrong" — carry evaluation without much descriptive content. You can call almost anything good or wrong without saying much about what it is. Thick concepts, by contrast, come loaded with both description and evaluation simultaneously. Words like *cruel*, *brave*, *treacherous*, *generous*, *lewd*, *gracious*, and *callous* all pick out recognisable features of the world while committing the speaker to an evaluative stance about those features.

Bernard Williams, who did the most to press this distinction in his 1985 book *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*, argued that thick concepts are in some ways more fundamental to moral life than thin ones. When we actually navigate ethical situations — deciding how to act, responding to others, holding people accountable — we reach for thick vocabulary. The thin terms come later, in philosophical reconstruction, when we step back and ask whether cruelty is "wrong" in some more general sense. But the thick terms are where the action is.

The standard picture of moral language, inherited from the emotivists and prescriptivists of the mid-twentieth century, wanted to separate the descriptive content from the evaluative attitude. On this view, "cruel" really means something like "causing needless suffering" (descriptive) plus an attitude of disapproval (evaluative). Strip the attitude away and you have a neutral empirical claim. Add the attitude back and you have a moral judgment. The two, on this picture, are logically separable.

Williams's challenge was to deny the separability. He argued that you cannot fully specify what counts as cruelty — which behaviours fall under the concept and which do not — without already having some evaluative understanding in view. The descriptive and evaluative components are, as he put it, "world-guided" and "action-guiding" at the same time, and you cannot cleanly peel one off the other.

## Why separability fails

The separability thesis faces a concrete problem: there is no purely descriptive account of what makes something cruel that does not already depend on evaluative sensitivity. Consider what distinguishes cruelty from mere harshness, or from the infliction of pain in surgery, or from the suffering caused by a difficult but fair disciplinary decision. The difference is not captured by any physical description of the act. It depends on context, intention, proportionality, and the relationship between the parties — features that require evaluative judgment to identify and weight correctly.

This is not a merely verbal point. It means that someone who lacks the relevant evaluative sensibility would not be able to reliably apply the concept, even if they had all the physical facts. They might misclassify acts that look harsh as cruel, or miss cruelty hiding behind polite form. The ability to use thick concepts well is itself a moral competency, not just a semantic one.

Philosophers like John McDowell pressed this further by arguing that thick concepts are a case of what he calls the "unity of value and fact" in ethics. To perceive that a situation is one of cruelty is to perceive something real about the world — something that a morally sensitive observer is equipped to see. This is part of what makes McDowell a **moral realist**: he thinks thick terms track genuine features of reality, not just features of the speaker's emotional state.

The separability thesis does retain some plausibility in extreme cases. One can imagine a sufficiently alienated observer — someone who simply does not care about suffering at all — who could pick out the extension of "cruel" by following social convention without internalising the evaluative commitment. But this is a parasitic case. The concept is constituted by its evaluative use; the alienated observer is free-riding on a practice they have not fully entered.

## The implications for moral argument

If thick concepts cannot be decomposed into a neutral description plus an added evaluation, this has consequences for how moral arguments work — and for what kind of evidence matters in ethics.

Debates about whether some practice is courageous or reckless, whether a policy is generous or paternalistic, whether a relationship is intimate or coercive, are not resolved by adding more factual information and then separately applying a moral filter. The evaluation is embedded in how we describe the situation. Getting the thick concept right is already doing ethical work. This suggests that much genuine moral disagreement is not disagreement about values in some abstract sense but disagreement about how to see a situation — how to apply the thick vocabulary that structures it.

This has practical relevance beyond the seminar room. When someone argues that a particular interrogation technique is "harsh but not cruel," they are doing something philosophically significant: they are contesting the application of a thick concept. The argument is not merely verbal. Settling it requires examining what cruelty is, what this technique actually does to a person, and whether the concept applies. Neither side can appeal to a purely neutral factual description that both parties already share and then argue about values separately.

It also matters for **the is-ought gap**, the famous Humean point that you cannot derive an evaluative conclusion from purely descriptive premises. If thick concepts exist, some apparently descriptive premises already carry evaluative load. This does not collapse the is-ought gap — it complicates it. It suggests the gap is not always where we think it is.

## Thick concepts and religious ethics

The thick/thin distinction is worth pressing in the context of religious moral discourse, where thick concepts do a great deal of unacknowledged work. Terms like *sacred*, *profane*, *impure*, *reverent*, and *blasphemous* are thick concepts — they describe and evaluate simultaneously. When a religious community describes an act as blasphemous, it is not reporting a neutral fact to which it then attaches an attitude of disapproval. The concept already structures what is being seen.

This creates a real difficulty for interfaith and cross-cultural moral dialogue. Someone outside the tradition may be able to identify which acts are called blasphemous by following social convention, but they cannot use the concept — cannot perceive what the concept picks out — without some degree of internalisation of the relevant evaluative sensibility. And if they lack that sensibility, they will resist the evaluative force of the term: it will seem like a description of nothing in particular plus an unjustified condemnation.

This is not a dismissal of religious thick concepts. It is an observation about their structure. The same applies to secular thick concepts that outsiders find opaque: "undignified" in certain bioethics contexts, "exploitative" in Marxist economic analysis, "alienating" in existentialist psychology. Each one rewards examination of its double nature.

## What this demands of moral discourse

One lesson from thick concepts is that the usual demand to "separate facts from values" in ethical debate is easier said than done. The instruction sounds like a methodological safeguard against bias, but it can obscure the degree to which the relevant facts are already seen through evaluatively laden vocabulary. Asking someone to state the facts about a situation without using any thick concepts often means asking them to redescribe it in a way that loses morally significant information.

This does not mean factual and evaluative claims are the same thing, or that ethics is reducible to description. It means that the relationship between them is more tangled than the clean separation model suggests. Careful moral reasoning involves working with thick concepts, examining whether they are being applied correctly, looking for cases where different thick concepts compete, and noticing when a redescription in different thick vocabulary changes what seems morally salient.

It also suggests a limit to a certain kind of moral progress narrative. Thinning out moral vocabulary — retreating to thin concepts like "preference satisfaction" or "welfare" — is sometimes presented as progress, as a way of achieving neutrality and cross-cultural agreement. But something is lost when thick concepts are dissolved. The moral texture of lived experience, the fine-grained discriminations that thick terms make possible, and the way they encode the accumulated evaluative knowledge of a community — all of this resists reduction to thin vocabulary without remainder.

Whether thick concepts ultimately support moral realism, as McDowell thinks, or whether they are better understood within some anti-realist framework, remains genuinely contested. What is not contested is that they exist, that they do real work in moral life, and that any account of ethics that ignores them is working with an impoverished picture of what moral language actually does.
