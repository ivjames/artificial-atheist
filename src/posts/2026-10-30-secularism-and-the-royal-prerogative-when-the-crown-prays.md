The world's oldest constitutional monarchies and several republics share an odd feature: their head of state carries explicit religious obligations written into law. This is not a relic that has been quietly hollowed out — in several cases it remains legally operative, shaping who may hold the office, what ceremonies mark its transfer, and whose faith is, by implication, the nation's own.

## What a confessional head of state actually means

A **confessional head of state** is one whose office is defined, at least in part, by membership in a specific religious tradition. The clearest living example is the British monarch, who must, under the Act of Settlement 1701 and subsequent legislation, be in communion with the Church of England and, since 1931, may not be or marry a Roman Catholic without losing succession rights. The coronation itself is a sacramental act: the anointing with oil, administered by the Archbishop of Canterbury, is theologically a consecration, not merely a ceremony.

This matters beyond symbolism. The monarch is the Supreme Governor of the Church of England — not merely its patron but its formal constitutional head. Bishops of the established church sit in the House of Lords as Lords Spiritual, their presence a direct downstream consequence of the monarch's religious office. The chain from a hereditary confessional requirement to legislative power is short and direct.

Norway retained a state church until 2017, when the Church of Norway was formally disestablished. Yet even after disestablishment, the Norwegian constitution still requires the monarch and more than half the Council of State to be members of the Church of Norway. Denmark's constitution similarly declares the Evangelical-Lutheran church to be the Danish folk church and the object of state support. In both cases, the office of head of state remains tethered to religious identity, even as the surrounding society has become one of the most secular in the world.

## The difference between ceremonial and operative religion

One common response is that these requirements are purely ceremonial — the anointing oil means nothing politically, and the Lords Spiritual are a minor anomaly in an otherwise functional legislature. This is worth taking seriously. In practice, the personal faith of a constitutional monarch has little effect on policy. The Supreme Governor does not write sermons, appoint bishops unilaterally, or veto synodal decisions. The role is largely formal.

But the distinction between ceremonial and operative religion does real work only when we are clear about what we mean by it. A ceremony that determines who may hold office is operative in the most direct sense: it excludes. A person who is not a baptised Christian in communion with the Church of England cannot be the British monarch, regardless of their qualifications, democratic popularity, or the will of Parliament expressed through ordinary statute. The exclusion is not ceremonial; it is structural.

The same point applies to oaths. Several states require an incoming head of state to swear before God, on a specific text, in a specifically religious frame. Where a secular alternative exists — as it does in Ireland and Germany — the requirement is genuinely ceremonial for those who choose the secular form. Where no alternative exists, the religious requirement is real. The distinction between the two cases is not trivial.

## How republican constitutions manage the same problem

Republics are not automatically free of this tension. Several explicitly Christian republics have constitutions whose preambles invoke God or a specific faith tradition, constraining — even if softly — the range of identities a head of state is expected to embody. The Irish constitution's preamble invokes the Holy Trinity and acknowledges Ireland's obligations to Jesus Christ. The Polish constitution's preamble references "those who believe in God as the source of truth, justice, good and beauty." These are not operative religious tests for office, but they frame the republic's self-understanding in confessional terms.

The practical effect is subtler than a formal test: it shapes the range of plausible candidates and raises the political cost of non-belief. No Irish president has been an open atheist. Whether this is coincidence, social pressure, or the filtering effect of a political culture shaped by Catholic social norms is impossible to cleanly separate — but the constitutional framing is not neutral.

France offers the sharpest republican contrast. The Fifth Republic's constitution declares France a **laïque** republic in its first article, with no religious invocation in the preamble. The president takes an oath of office before no deity, on no scripture. The office is constitutionally stripped of religious content. Sarkozy's personal Catholicism, Macron's public engagement with the Church — these are matters of individual belief, not constitutional function. France does not thereby solve the politics of religion, but it has at least severed the formal link between the office and any faith.

## Why reform is harder than it looks

Given that several of the most confessional constitutional arrangements exist in some of the most secular societies — the United Kingdom, Denmark, Norway — the obvious question is why reform is so slow. There are several reasons, and they deserve honest assessment rather than dismissal.

First, **legitimacy inertia**. Constitutional monarchy derives part of its political authority from continuity and tradition. Reform of the religious elements risks destabilising the broader settlement. Politicians who might otherwise support disestablishment quietly calculate that the symbolic return is small and the controversy real.

Second, **the bundling problem**. In the UK, disestablishment of the Church of England is bundled with questions about the Lords Spiritual, the monarchy's role, the coronation's legal status, and the broader devolution of religious affairs in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland, each with different church-state arrangements. Reforming one element pulls on all the others. This is not an argument against reform, but it explains the political economy of inaction.

Third, and most honestly, **popular indifference mixed with residual attachment**. Survey data consistently show that most British people are not practising Christians and do not attend church, yet also consistently show that majorities support retaining the monarchy and feel some attachment to the ceremonial dimension of the established church. People who believe nothing in particular can still prefer the aesthetic and communal continuity of a coronation with bishops over a modernised civic ceremony. Secularists often underestimate how much this residual attachment matters politically.

## What a secular alternative would require

A genuinely secular head of state arrangement would need to satisfy several conditions. The office must be open to citizens of any faith or none, without formal or informal penalty. Any oath of office must have a meaningful secular alternative that carries equal legal weight and no social stigma. The head of state must have no formal religious function — no role as governor, patron, or symbolic representative of a specific church. Public ceremonies marking the transfer of office should be civic in character, drawing on shared political values rather than sectarian theology.

None of this requires that the head of state be irreligious, or that religion be excluded from public life. A Catholic president taking the secular oath and attending Mass privately is perfectly consistent with a secular office. The requirement is structural neutrality, not personal irreligion.

Some modern monarchies have moved closer to this model without full disestablishment. The Swedish monarchy, after the 1999–2000 separation of church and state, no longer requires the monarch to be Lutheran, though Carl XVI Gustaf remains personally Lutheran. Sweden retained the monarchy while severing its confessional requirement — a path that shows the bundling problem is not insuperable.

## The deeper principle at stake

Confessional head-of-state arrangements raise a question that goes beyond politics: what does it mean for a democratic state to have an official religion mediated through its most visible representative? Even where the arrangement is largely ceremonial, it communicates something to citizens whose faith is not the official one — that they are guests in the national story rather than authors of it. A Jewish, Muslim, Hindu, or non-believing citizen of the United Kingdom can hold almost any office in the land, but not the one at the top. The exclusion is not enforced with hostility; it is enforced with tradition, which makes it harder, not easier, to contest.

Secular governance does not require that states be hostile to religion, or that public life be scrubbed of historical religious texture. It requires that the institutions of the state not be formally committed to the truth of any religious claim, and that the offices of the state be genuinely open to all citizens. A head of state whose legal existence depends on being the right kind of Christian falls short of that standard — regardless of how infrequently that fact intrudes on daily governance.

The argument for reform is not that the Church of England is false or that anointing oil is absurd. It is simpler: a democratic state's highest office should derive its authority from its citizens, and should be accessible to all of them. That principle has nothing to do with atheism and everything to do with equal standing before the law.
