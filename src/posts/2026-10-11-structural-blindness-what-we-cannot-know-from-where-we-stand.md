Epistemology has long asked what we can know and how we can justify it. A quieter question runs beneath that one: does *who you are* — your social position, your embodied experience, your place in a hierarchy — determine what you can know in the first place?

## The basic claim of standpoint epistemology

The core idea is not that knowledge is merely subjective. Standpoint theorists — Sandra Harding, Patricia Hill Collins, and Donna Haraway among the most cited — argue something more specific: that certain structural positions in society generate **epistemic advantage** for some questions, while other positions generate **epistemic disadvantage** or outright blindness. This is not the same as saying every perspective is equally valid. It is saying that perspectives are not epistemically neutral.

The intuition behind this has ordinary, non-theoretical support. A doctor who has never experienced chronic pain may understand its biology thoroughly while missing something important about how it reorganises a life. A manager who has never worked a warehouse floor may misread the flow of a shift in ways a picker would not. The knowledge gap here is not a failure of intelligence or effort. It is a gap produced by position. The standpoint theorist wants to explain why these gaps are systematic rather than random, and why they cluster around power.

## How position creates differential access

The mechanism matters. Position shapes attention. What you notice, what strikes you as requiring explanation, what you treat as unremarkable background — all of this is partly a function of what your daily life forces you to confront. Workers who deal with a system's failure points encounter data that planners rarely see. People subject to discrimination develop detailed, practically useful maps of social behaviour that those who benefit from the same discrimination rarely need to construct.

W.E.B. Du Bois described this as **double consciousness** — the experience of seeing oneself through the eyes of a dominant society while also holding one's own self-understanding. Whether or not one accepts his full theoretical framing, the observational point is hard to dismiss: being the object of a gaze, rather than its subject, teaches you things about that gaze that its wielder does not know. You learn the rules of a game more thoroughly when the rules are enforced against you.

This does not automatically translate into superior knowledge across all domains. A factory worker has structural access to facts about the production floor that an executive lacks, but neither has automatic epistemic advantage in, say, fluid dynamics. Standpoint claims are domain-specific. Where the standpoint theorist argues for advantage is in domains where social relations, power, and lived experience are directly at issue.

## The strong and weak readings

There is a version of standpoint epistemology that is defensible and a version that collapses into incoherence. Keeping them apart matters.

The **weak reading** holds that social position is a relevant variable in assessing testimony and inquiry. Research on racial bias in medicine conducted entirely by researchers with no exposure to the patient population being studied may have methodological blind spots worth identifying. Sociology that treats the lives of the powerful as the default and the lives of the marginalised as variation to be explained may be asking the wrong questions. On this reading, standpoint considerations function as a corrective heuristic: look for whose perspective is absent, whose experience was not consulted, what the inquiry took for granted. This is compatible with standard scientific norms and actually reinforces them.

The **strong reading** holds that members of a subordinated group have privileged — or even exclusive — epistemic access to truths about that group's experience, and that outsiders cannot meaningfully evaluate those claims. This version has serious problems. It risks making social inquiry circular: only those inside a position can evaluate claims about it, which insulates those claims from the kind of external scrutiny that makes knowledge reliable. It also assumes that membership in a social category produces uniform experience, which it does not. Two people in structurally similar positions may interpret their situation in radically different ways; the standpoint framework gives no principled way to adjudicate between them without appealing to criteria that transcend the standpoint itself.

Harding's own response to this — what she called **strong objectivity** — attempts a middle path. The claim is that actively seeking out marginalised standpoints produces *more* objective inquiry, not less, because it exposes assumptions that otherwise go unexamined. Whether this resolves the tension or merely restates it is a live debate.

## The hermeneutical injustice connection

Miranda Fricker's concept of **hermeneutical injustice** connects the standpoint debate to concrete epistemic harm. Hermeneutical injustice occurs when a gap in collective interpretive resources puts someone at an unfair disadvantage in making sense of their own social experience. The concept of sexual harassment did not exist as a named, shared category until feminists laboured to create it. Before that, the experience was real — women suffered it, described it haltingly, were often disbelieved — but there was no shared conceptual tool adequate to the phenomenon.

This is a standpoint problem with a clear mechanism. Those who were not subject to the experience had no practical incentive to develop the concept. Those who were subject to it lacked the social power to make their incipient concepts stick. The resulting gap was not random; it tracked power. And it had real consequences: women who tried to report or resist what we now call harassment were often dismissed as confused, oversensitive, or lying, partly because the conceptual apparatus for understanding their claim was underdeveloped.

The standpoint insight here is that conceptual resources are themselves distributed unevenly, and that distribution follows structural lines. Generating adequate concepts requires access to experience, and access to experience is shaped by position.

## Where the argument encounters friction

The strongest objection to standpoint epistemology is not that position is irrelevant but that the theory risks **epistemic balkanisation** — a world in which knowledge claims are always evaluated partly by the identity of the claimant, and in which the content of a claim can be dismissed by pointing at the speaker's social location rather than examining the argument.

This is not a hypothetical risk. In practice, "you can't understand this because you haven't lived it" is sometimes used to shut down legitimate scrutiny. When a challenge to an empirical claim is met with a claim about the challenger's standpoint, rather than with evidence or argument, something has gone epistemically wrong. The ad hominem and the genetic fallacy are not made valid by being deployed in the service of marginalized groups. Arguments stand or fall on their merits, and the person who points out a flaw in a study on housing discrimination is not automatically suspect because they are housed.

Standpoint theorists are often aware of this objection and try to pre-empt it. The response is typically that standpoint considerations bear on which *questions* get asked, which *evidence* gets collected, and whose *testimony* is taken seriously — not on the formal validity of arguments. That is a reasonable demarcation. But it requires a level of precision in application that is not always present in actual standpoint discourse, where the line between methodological sensitivity and motivated dismissal can blur.

## What standpoint epistemology actually demands of inquiry

Taken at its most defensible, standpoint epistemology is a set of methodological cautions rather than a replacement for evidence-based reasoning. It asks: who designed this study, and what did their position lead them to take for granted? Whose testimony was weighted most heavily, and why? What questions were not asked because no one with a stake in asking them was in the room?

These are not rhetorical questions. They have practical, corrective force. The history of medical research provides an obvious case: for decades, clinical trials were conducted predominantly on male subjects, and findings were generalised to women without adequate testing. This was not malice; it was the product of a structural assumption so pervasive it was invisible to most of the researchers involved. Standpoint considerations, in retrospect, would have flagged the problem earlier.

The same logic applies to inquiry about religion, belief, and disbelief — the concerns at the heart of this publication. Scholarship on religious experience conducted entirely by committed believers may miss dimensions that matter. Equally, scholarship conducted entirely by those with a prior commitment to debunking may fail to accurately characterise what believers report experiencing and why. The corrective is not to prioritise one standpoint but to insist that inquiry remain genuinely open to what each position can see that the other cannot.

What standpoint epistemology does not do — and should not be asked to do — is replace the evaluation of evidence with the evaluation of identities. Position shapes the likelihood of noticing certain things. It does not determine whether what is noticed is real or whether the conclusions drawn from it are valid. Those questions still require argument, evidence, and the willingness to be wrong. No standpoint exempts anyone from that.
