Adoption law sits at one of the sharpest edges in the secular state's self-understanding. It involves children who cannot advocate for themselves, prospective parents whose fitness the state must assess, and agencies — many of them religious — that the state licenses to do that assessing on its behalf. When a faith-based agency declines to place a child with a same-sex couple, a single atheist, or an interfaith household on doctrinal grounds, the question is not merely one of tolerance or pluralism. It is a structural question: can the state delegate a public function to a private actor and then exempt that actor from the nondiscrimination rules that govern public functions?

## What adoption agencies actually do

Private adoption agencies, including religiously affiliated ones, are not simply private clubs exercising the right of association. They perform a quasi-governmental function. In most jurisdictions they are licensed by the state, receive public funding in whole or in part, work with children who are wards of the state or whose cases are processed through state courts, and submit to regulatory oversight covering home studies, background checks, and record-keeping. The state cannot place every child itself; it relies on a network of intermediary agencies to extend its capacity.

This delegation matters enormously for how we should think about religious exemptions. A church that restricts its own membership, a religious school that hires only co-religionists for theology instruction, or a faith community that refuses to perform marriages outside its tradition — these are institutions acting within their own sphere, on their own resources, for their own purposes. A licensed adoption agency placing children from the state's care is doing something different. It exercises a public function with public consequences for identifiable third parties: the children awaiting placement, and the prospective parents screened out by religious criteria the state itself does not apply.

## The strongest case for religious exemptions

The argument for allowing faith-based agencies to apply their own criteria is not frivolous, and a fair treatment of the question requires engaging it seriously. The core of it runs as follows: religious adoption agencies, particularly Catholic Charities affiliates and evangelical Protestant networks, built much of the infrastructure of child welfare in the twentieth century. They have long track records, trained social workers, established foster networks, and community relationships that the state would struggle to replicate quickly. If the state forces these agencies to choose between their doctrinal commitments and their licences, the agencies close — and that means fewer placements, longer waits, and children who suffer the administrative gap.

A secondary argument appeals to parental matching. Some prospective adoptive parents specifically seek a religious agency because they want a process conducted within their tradition; forcing agencies to accept all applicants regardless of the agency's character, this argument goes, homogenises provision and removes legitimate choice. A third argument invokes general religious liberty: if the state compels a religious organisation to act against its sincere convictions as the price of participating in civil society, it effectively excludes religious actors from public life — which looks like a form of discrimination in its own right.

These arguments are worth taking seriously. But they do not resolve the structural problem; they merely restate it in sympathetic terms.

## Why the exemption argument fails on its own logic

Start with the infrastructure argument. If a private actor provides an essential public service and the state has grown dependent on that provision, the conclusion cannot be that the state is therefore obligated to exempt the actor from generally applicable rules. That gets the logic exactly backwards. The state's dependence on a particular provider is a reason to build alternative provision, not a reason to grant the provider a regulatory carve-out. The argument from indispensability, pressed hard enough, would allow any sufficiently large contractor to dictate its own terms to the government — which is simply capture dressed as accommodation.

The matching argument fares no better. Prospective parents are entitled to seek out an agency whose culture suits them; they are not entitled to have the state licence an agency that discriminates against other applicants using criteria the state itself has deemed impermissible. There is a difference between an agency with a distinctive ethos — a preference for certain kinds of counselling, a particular approach to open adoption, a religious framing of the process — and an agency that maintains an exclusion list. The former is a matter of institutional character. The latter is an eligibility rule.

The religious liberty argument is the most serious, but it conflates two things. Requiring a religious organisation not to discriminate when performing a licensed public function is not the same as compelling it to profess beliefs it rejects. Catholic Charities is not being asked to endorse same-sex marriage; it is being told that if it wants a state licence to place children, it must use the same eligibility criteria the state uses. The organisation retains every freedom to teach, to worship, to operate within its community. It does not retain the right to carry a state licence on its own doctrinal terms.

## The child's interest and the question of best placement

One consideration sometimes dropped from this debate is the child. Adoption law in every liberal jurisdiction formally centres the best interest of the child as the governing principle. If an agency's religious criteria exclude a category of prospective parent, children who might have been placed with those parents are either placed elsewhere (with longer waits) or remain in care longer. The harm is probabilistic but real. Every month a child spends in foster or institutional care rather than a permanent family home has documented developmental costs.

This does not mean every faith-based agency is placing children badly — many are doing excellent work by any measurable standard. But it does mean that any regulatory exemption carries a cost that is borne not by the adults arguing about liberty, but by children who have no voice in the argument. A secular framework takes that asymmetry seriously. The child has not chosen to have their placement options narrowed by an agency's theological commitments.

## What a genuinely neutral framework looks like

**Secular neutrality** in this context does not mean hostility to religious agencies. It means that the state's licensing criteria, funding conditions, and nondiscrimination rules apply uniformly, regardless of an agency's religious character. An agency may be staffed entirely by members of one faith, may frame its casework in explicitly religious terms, and may be embedded in a religious community — none of that triggers any disqualification. What disqualifies is the imposition of eligibility criteria on prospective parents that the state itself would not apply.

Several jurisdictions have tried to find middle ground — for example, requiring that an agency that cannot place a child with a particular applicant must refer that applicant to another agency without delay or stigma. This is sometimes called the "seamless referral" approach. It is a reasonable administrative compromise, but it has a structural weakness: the referring agency still decides who gets referred, the referral itself communicates a judgement, and in areas with thin provision the alternative agency may simply not exist. A referral is not the same as placement.

The cleaner model — though politically harder — is to condition public licences and public funding on full nondiscrimination compliance, while making no restriction on religiously affiliated agencies that operate purely on private funds without a state licence. This preserves genuine institutional pluralism: religious communities may run their own internal programmes on their own terms. What they may not do is borrow the state's authority and the state's money while applying rules the state has decided are impermissible.

## The broader principle this case illustrates

The adoption case is a particularly clear instance of a recurring problem in secular governance: the state increasingly delivers services through third-party providers — charities, faith groups, community organisations — and the question of whether those providers carry the state's legal obligations with them has no settled answer. Courts in the United Kingdom, the United States, and across the European Union have reached different conclusions, sometimes within the same decade.

What the adoption context shows, with unusual clarity, is that the question cannot be resolved by appealing to religious liberty alone or to nondiscrimination alone. It requires asking, for each case: is the actor exercising a genuinely private function, or has it taken on a public function with public resources? If the latter, the state's equality norms travel with the delegation. The religious character of the provider changes the cultural texture of what is delivered; it does not change the legal character of what is being done.

Secularism, properly understood, does not demand that religious organisations disappear from civil society. It demands that the state not use religious organisations as a way of insulating discriminatory practices from the scrutiny those practices would face if the state applied them directly. Children waiting for families deserve the full range of eligible homes the society can provide. They do not deserve a market shaped by theological criteria that no secular court would find relevant to their welfare.
