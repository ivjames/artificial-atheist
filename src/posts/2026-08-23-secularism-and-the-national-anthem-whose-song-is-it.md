National anthems are not merely songs. They are legal and ceremonial instruments through which a state declares something about itself to its own citizens and to the world. When that declaration includes the name of a god, a prayer, or an act of religious submission, a secular state has a problem it cannot dissolve by looking the other way.

## The scale of the problem

The majority of the world's national anthems contain explicit religious content. A 2019 analysis of all 193 UN member state anthems found that roughly 60 percent invoke a deity by name, request divine protection, or frame the nation's existence in terms of providential purpose. Among established liberal democracies, the proportion is still striking. The United Kingdom's "God Save the King" is a direct prayer. Germany's third stanza of "Das Lied der Deutschen," no longer sung officially, contained theological overtones, though the reinstated first line — "Einigkeit und Recht und Freiheit" — is secular. The United States has no law requiring citizens to sing "The Star-Spangled Banner," but its fourth stanza, rarely performed, invokes divine trust. Canada's "O Canada" was amended in 2018 to remove a gender reference but retained "God keep our land." These are not trivial lyrical quirks. They are state-endorsed speech at the highest ceremonial level.

## What secular neutrality actually demands

Secularism, understood as **state neutrality toward religion**, does not require that the state be hostile to religious expression. It requires that the state not use its authority to endorse, coerce, or privilege a particular religious position. An anthem sung at state occasions — parliamentary openings, military ceremonies, sporting events with government sponsorship, citizenship ceremonies — is precisely such an exercise of state authority. When the state requires its officials, its military, its new citizens to stand and participate in a song addressed to a god, it is doing something qualitatively different from permitting religious expression in the public square. It is performing that expression itself.

The strongest counterargument here is that the religious language in many anthems is vestigial. "God Save the King" began as a Jacobite-era loyalty song; its theology, the argument goes, has been drained of content through centuries of rote repetition. Most people singing it are not praying. But this argument proves too much. If the words have no religious meaning, they have no religious value either — and the case for retaining them weakens. If they do retain meaning, then the state is endorsing that meaning. The vestigial defence cannot have it both ways.

## The citizenship ceremony as a hard case

The sharpest pressure point is the **citizenship ceremony**. When a person takes on a new nationality, the state is not merely asking them to fill out a form. It is asking them to perform membership — to stand with fellow citizens and affirm belonging. In countries whose citizenship ceremonies include the national anthem, an atheist, a religious minority, or someone whose faith prohibits the veneration of a national symbol faces a coercive choice: participate in words they find false or exclusionary, or visibly dissent at the moment designed to mark inclusion.

Australia's "Advance Australia Fair" was amended in 2021 to acknowledge Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in its second line, but the anthem still refers to "boundless plains to share" under an implicitly providential framework, and the ceremony retains strong normative pressure to sing. France, whose laïcité is among the most rigorous secular frameworks in the world, has "La Marseillaise" — which contains no direct reference to God and is arguably the world's most successfully secular anthem. The French case is instructive: the absence of religious language was not accidental. The anthem emerged from a revolutionary context explicitly hostile to theocratic governance, and that origin shaped its text in ways that still serve secular purposes today.

## Why "tradition" is not a neutral defence

When challenged on the religious content of an anthem, governments almost invariably retreat to tradition. The language has been there for centuries; it is part of national identity; changing it would be divisive. Each of these claims deserves scrutiny.

**Tradition as such** does not confer legitimacy in a democratic framework. Many traditions have been revised because they excluded or subordinated groups — the inclusion of women in oaths of office, the removal of racially coded language from civic documents, the revision of marriage law. The question is not how old the tradition is but whether it continues to serve the values the state claims to hold. A state that formally commits to equal treatment of citizens regardless of religion cannot coherently also maintain that its central civic song is permanently exempt from that standard.

The **divisiveness** argument is more interesting. Changing an anthem does risk fracture along cultural and generational lines, and a secular state committed to democratic legitimacy ought to take that risk seriously. But it is worth noting that the divisiveness cuts both ways: retaining explicitly religious language in state ceremonies is itself divisive for the growing proportion of citizens who identify as nonreligious or who belong to minority faiths. In the United Kingdom, "no religion" is now the largest single category in the census. The anthem that supposedly unites the nation is, for a plurality of its citizens, the voice of a faith they do not share.

## The redesign options and what they signal

States that have grappled seriously with this issue have pursued different strategies. **Lyrical amendment** — replacing or removing religious phrases — is the most direct approach. "O Canada" shows it is possible without triggering national collapse. Rwanda's anthem, adopted in 2002 after the genocide, was deliberately composed to emphasise unity and reconstruction over religion. **Alternative verses** have been proposed in several countries as an opt-in for secular occasions, but these tend to institutionalise a two-tier civic identity rather than resolve the underlying tension.

**Selective use** — performing the anthem only at some state occasions and not others — achieves a practical workaround but leaves the symbolic problem intact. **New anthems** are rarely adopted because they require a political consensus that rarely forms in stable democracies; the anthem question tends to move only after a significant political rupture, such as a new constitution or post-conflict reconstruction.

None of these options is costless. That is precisely the point. Secular states that treat this as a non-issue are not avoiding a cost; they are imposing one silently on religious minorities and nonbelievers, who are expected to perform civic belonging through language that marks them as outsiders.

## What an honest secular state would do

An honest secular state would begin by acknowledging that the anthem question is not trivial or merely aesthetic. It would ask, as it does with other questions of state neutrality, whether its ceremonial practices treat all citizens equally regardless of their relationship to religion. It would not insulate the anthem from that scrutiny simply because the anthem is old or because revision would be politically inconvenient.

That does not mean every state with a religious anthem must immediately amend it. Democratic legitimacy matters, and a change imposed without broad consent would itself violate the procedural values that underpin secular governance. But it does mean that secular states should be willing to have the conversation openly, count the nonreligious among the stakeholders whose interests are at issue, and resist the comfortable fiction that tradition and neutrality are the same thing. The song a state sings about itself tells its citizens something about whose country it really is. That is worth taking seriously.
