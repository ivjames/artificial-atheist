The secular state promises neutrality toward religion. It will not favor one faith over another, will not punish belief as such, and will not treat membership in a religious community as grounds for suspicion. That promise is straightforward when the state is passing laws or licensing marriages. It becomes acutely difficult when the state is running an intelligence agency.

## What makes religious surveillance different

Domestic intelligence work always involves monitoring people who have not yet committed crimes. The entire logic of counterterrorism and counterextremism is prospective: identify threats before they materialize. That prospective stance collides directly with religious freedom, because the observable markers of radicalization — prayer frequency, doctrinal intensity, organizational affiliation, the books someone reads — are also just markers of ordinary, protected religious practice.

Surveillance is not neutral when its trigger is religious behavior. The FBI's COINTELPRO program in the 1960s monitored the Nation of Islam alongside explicitly violent groups, treating the theology as evidence enough for investigation. The NYPD Demographics Unit, active from roughly 2003 to 2014, mapped Muslim neighborhoods, mosques, and student associations across the northeastern United States, not because of any specific threat intelligence but because of religious and ethnic identity. British MI5 guidance leaked in 2009 acknowledged that analysts used "Salafi" as a proxy category, despite the fact that the vast majority of Salafi Muslims have no involvement in political violence. In each case, the intelligence apparatus was doing something the legislature would never be permitted to do openly: treating membership in a religious tradition as a risk factor.

## The structural problem with proxy categories

Intelligence agencies rarely admit to monitoring religion as such. The standard defense is that they are monitoring **ideology**, not faith — that the subject of interest is a political program dressed in religious language, not personal belief or worship. This distinction does real work in some cases. There is a genuine difference between tracking a network that raises funds for a designated terrorist organization and cataloguing who attends Friday prayers. But the distinction collapses in practice because the ideological markers analysts are trained to spot are, in most cases, indistinguishable from religious practice at its most devout.

Consider the concept of **Islamist** as an analytical category. The term is meant to capture Muslims who seek to organize political life according to Islamic law, but it stretches across an enormous range of positions — from genuinely violent movements like ISIS to democratic parties like Ennahda in Tunisia that have contested elections, accepted defeats, and voluntarily relinquished power. When an analyst marks someone as "Islamist-adjacent" because they attend a mosque associated with the Muslim Brotherhood's historical network, the religious-ideological line has been erased entirely. The same problem arises with Christian nationalist movements in the United States: the analytical community has been far slower to develop proxy categories for white evangelical political organizing, which itself raises the question of whether the double standard is ideological or reflects genuine differential threat assessments — and how one would know.

## The secularism principle as a constraint on intelligence

A committed secularism does more than prohibit state churches. It holds that the state must not treat citizens differently on the basis of their religious identity without compelling, evidence-based justification. Applied to intelligence, this generates a clear procedural requirement: **religious identity or practice cannot be a threshold criterion** for opening an investigation. Evidence of a specific harm — fundraising for a proscribed group, communications with a known operative, acquisition of materials with no plausible lawful use — can justify surveillance. Attending a particular mosque, reading a particular theologian, or expressing that secular law is inferior to divine law cannot, on its own.

This is not a naive position. Some ideological commitments do predict a higher base rate of political violence than others, and intelligence services are in the business of acting on probabilistic information. But probability estimates derived from religious category membership are both epistemically weak and politically corrosive. They are weak because the false positive rate is enormous: the overwhelming majority of people who hold any given theological position, however extreme by liberal standards, do not engage in or support violence. They are corrosive because the surveillance itself — once it becomes known, and it always eventually becomes known — degrades the relationship between the monitored community and the state, making community members less willing to report genuine concerns, which is the most reliable source of actionable intelligence.

The paradox is tight: intelligence-led approaches that rely on religious profiling are not only a secularism problem, they are a security problem. The UK's Prevent programme has faced sustained criticism precisely on these grounds. Researchers including Arun Kundnani have documented how Prevent's focus on Muslim communities as a risk category produced widespread distrust and caused community members to withhold information from exactly the authorities Prevent was designed to empower. A secular approach is not just more principled — it is, by the evidence, more effective.

## How secular constraints are supposed to be enforced

Democratic states typically address this problem through a combination of legal oversight, parliamentary or congressional committee review, and judicial warrant requirements. In theory, these mechanisms enforce the secularism principle by requiring that surveillance be predicated on individualized suspicion rather than group membership. In practice, the mechanisms have significant gaps.

Warrant requirements apply to the interception of communications content, but most demographic mapping — recording who attends which mosque, which community center, which religious bookshop — involves what lawyers call **public-space collection**, which requires no warrant at all. Parliamentary oversight committees generally receive classified briefings rather than independently audit operations; they are structurally dependent on the agencies they oversee for the information they would need to identify abuses. And whistleblower protections are notoriously thin in intelligence contexts, meaning that internal dissenters face severe personal costs for surfacing concerns.

The legal frameworks most likely to enforce secular neutrality in intelligence work are civil liberties litigation and freedom of information requests pursued after the fact. The NYPD Demographics Unit was ultimately dismantled partly in response to litigation by the American Civil Liberties Union and the Associated Press's sustained investigative reporting. These are valuable correction mechanisms, but they operate years after the surveillance has occurred and after communities have absorbed the damage.

## The harder question: ideology that targets the secular state

The most difficult edge case for secular intelligence doctrine is when the ideology under scrutiny is explicitly anti-secular — when it holds that the secular constitutional order is itself illegitimate and should be replaced by theocratic governance, and when some adherents of that view have endorsed or engaged in violence toward that end.

The secular state is, in this situation, being asked to tolerate the expression of views that, if acted upon, would end secularism. This is a genuine dilemma, not a puzzle with an easy liberal answer. Karl Popper's **paradox of tolerance** is relevant here: a tolerant society that tolerates all positions unconditionally will eventually be captured by intolerant movements. The question is when, precisely, the state is justified in moving from passive toleration to active monitoring to legal suppression.

The defensible secular answer distinguishes sharply between speech, organization, and action. Holding that sharia is the only legitimate law is a belief; publishing that view is speech; raising money for organizations that pursue that goal through elections is lawful political organization; providing material support to groups that pursue it through violence is a crime. Intelligence work is justified at the last stage and, with careful predication, in the organizational stage when there is specific evidence of criminality. It is not justified at the level of belief or speech, even when those beliefs are hostile to the secular settlement that makes the protection of dissent possible.

This line is genuinely uncomfortable. It means the secular state must extend its protections to movements that would abolish those protections if they obtained power. But the alternative — treating ideological opposition to secularism as sufficient grounds for surveillance — collapses the principle entirely. A secularism that applies only to beliefs that endorse secularism is not a principle; it is a preference for a particular tribe.

## What rigorous secular neutrality actually requires

The practical implications are more demanding than most intelligence reform discussions acknowledge. They require agencies to develop **individualized predication standards** that explicitly exclude religious identity, affiliation, and practice as threshold criteria. They require oversight bodies with genuine independence and access to operational data, not just briefing summaries. They require sunset provisions on surveillance authorities so that programs do not persist on institutional inertia after their evidentiary basis has eroded. And they require the same analytical frameworks to apply across religious communities — if attending a mosque whose leadership has expressed sympathy for political violence is a reportable marker, then attending a church whose leadership has expressed sympathy for political violence must be treated identically.

None of this resolves the hard cases. Intelligence is inherently probabilistic, inherently operating on incomplete information, and inherently operating before harm occurs. The secular constraint does not eliminate the difficulty; it specifies the conditions under which the difficulty must be navigated. It rules out the shortcut of treating religious identity as a reliable signal, not because that shortcut never produces any useful information but because the cost — to civil equality, to the community-state relationship, and to the principle that the state does not see its citizens through the lens of their faith — is too high, and the benefit too thin, to justify it.

The secular state's promise of neutrality is, in the end, most meaningful precisely where it is most inconvenient. Intelligence agencies operate with wide discretion, classified information, and institutional cultures that reward risk reduction over civil liberties scrupulousness. Enforcing secular neutrality in that environment requires structural mechanisms, not just principled statements. Without them, the promise remains what it has too often been: a rule that applies until the state decides it is afraid.
