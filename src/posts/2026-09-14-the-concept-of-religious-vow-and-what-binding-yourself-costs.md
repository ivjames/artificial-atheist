The religious vow is one of the oldest technologies of commitment humanity has devised. It is not merely a promise; it is a promise made sacred, witnessed by the divine, and treated as categorically different from any ordinary contract.

## What a vow actually claims to do

A standard promise binds two parties and can, in principle, be dissolved by mutual agreement. A vow is structurally different. It invokes a third party — God, or the cosmos, or the community as a transcendent entity — who does not release the vower from obligation simply because circumstances change. The vower is, in the vocabulary of most traditions, not free to renegotiate. This is the point. The whole apparatus of the vow is designed to make exit costly, or in some formulations, metaphysically impossible.

In Catholic canon law, solemn religious vows — taken by monks, nuns, and members of religious orders — are distinguished from simple vows precisely by their irresolvability under ordinary conditions. A solemn vow of chastity, poverty, and obedience does not dissolve when the person who took it changes her mind. Only the Pope can dispense from certain solemn vows, and even then the theological tradition debates whether the underlying obligation fully lapses or merely becomes unenforceable. In Jewish law, the tractate *Nedarim* devotes substantial analysis to vows (*nedarim*) and their annulment, with elaborate procedures for rabbinic release — suggesting that the tradition simultaneously treated vows as binding and recognised that binding oneself too tightly was itself a spiritual problem. The Talmud records sages who advised against vowing at all.

## The problem of the future self

The philosophical difficulty at the heart of the vow is not unique to religion, but religion sharpens it. When a twenty-two-year-old novice takes permanent vows, she is making a commitment that will govern the behaviour of a forty-five-year-old woman, a sixty-year-old woman, and an eighty-year-old woman she has not yet become. The question is whether the young novice has the authority to bind those future selves.

Derek Parfit's work on personal identity is useful here, even though he did not write about religious vows. If personal identity is not as deep as we ordinarily assume — if the self at twenty-two and the self at sixty are related by overlapping chains of psychological continuity rather than strict numerical identity — then the authority of a past self to bind a future one becomes genuinely uncertain. The vow-taker and the vow-bearer may not be the same person in any philosophically robust sense.

Religious traditions are not unaware of this problem; they simply answer it differently. Most invoke the soul as the stable substrate that persists beneath psychological change. The soul that vowed is the same soul that must keep the vow, regardless of how much the personality, beliefs, or desires of the person have shifted. This is a coherent answer, but it depends entirely on a contested metaphysical claim. If there is no soul in the relevant sense — if the self is constituted by psychological and neural processes that genuinely change — then the authority of the vow to bind across decades looks much weaker.

## Consent and the conditions of taking a vow

Even setting aside questions of identity over time, the conditions under which vows are typically taken raise concerns about the quality of consent. Most permanent religious vows are taken in early adulthood, often after years of formation within an institution that has a strong interest in the novice completing profession. The novice has been immersed in a world with its own language, its own emotional logic, and its own account of what fulfilment looks like. She has likely had limited exposure to serious alternatives during the formation period.

This is not a charge of brainwashing. Formation programs vary enormously, and many include deliberate exposure to doubt, to the outside world, and to the voices of those who left. But **structural pressure** and **explicit coercion** exist on a spectrum, and the conditions of novitiate formation sit at a point on that spectrum that warrants scrutiny. A person who has spent three years being told that God is calling her to this life, surrounded by people who have made the same commitment, embedded in a daily rhythm that makes the vow feel like the only natural conclusion — that person is not choosing in the same unconstrained sense as someone selecting a job or a city.

The same concern applies to vows taken in other contexts. Hasidic marriages, where the parties often have limited pre-marital contact and strong communal expectations, involve an implicit or explicit acceptance of a framework that will govern the rest of one's life. The vow of the Nazirite in the Hebrew Bible was taken voluntarily but within a culture that gave it enormous social weight. Voluntary does not automatically mean free in any meaningful sense.

## When the vow becomes harmful

The test of any commitment mechanism is not how it functions when things go well but what it does when they go badly. Religious traditions have developed some safety valves. Catholic canon law allows laicisation — a process by which a priest can be released from his vows and returned to lay status, though it is lengthy and was deliberately made more difficult under John Paul II. Religious sisters can petition for dispensation. The mechanisms exist.

But the existence of a mechanism does not mean it functions equitably. Accounts from former members of religious orders document years-long processes, requests that are denied or indefinitely delayed, and the experience of being told that a desire to leave is itself a spiritual failure — evidence of weakness, temptation, or insufficient faith. This framing turns the subjective experience of misery into proof that one should stay, which is a self-sealing structure. The person who is suffering is suffering because she lacks the virtue to honour her commitment, and the solution is more commitment. The vow, in these cases, does not protect the vower; it traps her.

The vow of celibacy in Catholic priesthood provides the starkest contemporary example. The Church has faced credible evidence, from its own internal investigations, that enforced celibacy has in some cases contributed to psychological damage, double lives, and in the worst instances, the abuse of vulnerable people. This is not an argument that celibacy is inherently harmful — many people live celibate lives freely and flourish. It is an argument that **permanent irrevocable commitment to celibacy**, extracted at an early age under significant institutional pressure, creates conditions that a proportion of people simply cannot sustain, with consequences that fall not just on the vower but on others.

## The secular parallel and what it illuminates

It is worth noting that secular life has its own commitment mechanisms, and they carry some of the same risks. Marriage vows, in their traditional form, invoke permanence. Oaths of military service bind the person in ways that can be very difficult to exit. Long-term contracts in professional and financial life constrain future choice. The vow is not a uniquely religious pathology.

What religion adds is the claim that the obligation is not merely social or contractual but **cosmically real**. Breaking a vow is not just a breach of contract; it is an offence against God, a defilement of the soul, a rupture in one's relationship with the sacred. This additional layer of obligation — invisible, unverifiable, but emotionally and psychologically real for the believer — is what distinguishes the religious vow from a secular commitment. It is also what makes the vow's hold so much harder to escape, because exit involves not just social cost but the terror of divine punishment or the grief of felt betrayal of God.

For a non-believer, this additional layer is simply not there. The vow is a promise, full stop, and promises can be ethically released when circumstances change sufficiently. This does not make promise-keeping unimportant — it makes it important for the right reasons, namely the interests of actual people rather than the commands of an unseen witness. That reframing has practical consequences: it makes the question of whether someone should be held to a vow into a question about human welfare, which is tractable, rather than a question about divine will, which is not.

## What traditions get right

None of this is to say that the institution of the vow is without value. Commitment mechanisms exist because human beings are notoriously bad at acting on their long-term preferences in the face of short-term temptation. A person who vows to dedicate her life to contemplation, service, or community is doing something psychologically real and potentially valuable: she is using the weight of commitment to protect a choice she knows she might otherwise abandon when things get hard. There is wisdom in recognising that the self is not a reliable executor of its own best intentions.

The traditions that have thought longest about vows — and the Talmudic discussion is a good example — also recognise the danger of vowing too readily. The instinct to say "I will always do this" or "I will never do that" is powerful but often unwise, because it assumes a knowledge of future circumstances and a stability of future preference that we rarely possess. The Jewish legal tradition's ambivalence about vowing, and its elaborate machinery for annulment, reflects a mature understanding that permanent commitments are both powerful and perilous.

The honest position is that vows can be appropriate structures for appropriate commitments, freely made by people with genuine understanding of what they are undertaking — and that the religious version of the vow adds claims that are not only unverifiable but that systematically disadvantage the person who later needs to exit. The divine witness who never speaks cannot release you from your obligation. That asymmetry is not a feature; it is a flaw.
