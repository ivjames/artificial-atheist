Epistemology's most persistent internal dispute is not about whether we can know things, but about what makes a belief count as justified in the first place. The answer turns entirely on where you think justification lives.

## The basic divide

**Internalism** holds that what justifies a belief must be something the believer has cognitive access to — reasons, evidence, logical relations among ideas. If you can't, even in principle, reflect on the factor that is supposedly doing the justifying, then that factor doesn't justify anything for you. The intuition behind this is strong: justification seems like something you should be able to defend. If a belief is justified, surely you ought to be able to say *why*.

**Externalism** denies this. What matters, on this view, is not whether you can articulate your grounds but whether your belief is produced by a process that reliably tracks the truth, or whether you stand in the right kind of relationship to the facts. You can be justified without knowing that you are, and you can fail to be justified even while feeling fully confident in your reasons. The externalist insists that justification is a feature of how your beliefs connect to reality, not just a feature of how things look from inside your head.

Neither position is obviously correct. Each captures something real about the concept of justification, and the tension between them reappears constantly in debates about religious belief, testimony, moral knowledge, and scientific method.

## What internalism gets right

The strongest argument for internalism is what is sometimes called the **guidance condition**. Epistemology is supposed to tell us how to form beliefs well. If the factors that justify a belief are, by definition, inaccessible to the believer, then knowing about them cannot guide us toward better believing. A theory of justification that cannot inform practice seems to have abandoned part of its job.

Consider a simple example. You believe that a particular building is safe because it looks sturdy. An internalist asks: what are your actual grounds? You have visual impressions, perhaps some background knowledge about construction, maybe a memory of having seen similar buildings. These are the things that should do the justificatory work, because these are the things you can bring to bear on the question. If it turns out that the building actually *is* unsafe — that your impressions were misleading — the internalist doesn't automatically say your belief was unjustified. You might have had perfectly good reasons given what was available to you.

This connects to a deep moral intuition: we don't generally blame people for acting on their best available reasons, even when those reasons turn out to be wrong. Justification, on the internalist picture, is an *evaluation of the believer's epistemic conduct*, not a verdict on whether they happened to get lucky with the facts.

## What externalism gets right

The strongest argument for externalism is the **reliability problem** with pure internalism. Imagine a person raised in an environment of systematic deception — a Descartes-style evil-demon scenario, or just a community that has been given consistently false information about history. This person's internal evidence may be impeccable. Their reasons cohere beautifully. Their beliefs are formed carefully, with attention to what they take to be good grounds. And yet every belief is false, produced by a mechanism utterly disconnected from truth.

Are those beliefs *justified*? An internalist seems committed to saying yes. But that conclusion strikes many philosophers as wrong, or at least as revealing that internalist justification is not the only thing epistemically worth caring about. Something has gone missing: the relationship between the belief-forming process and the truth.

Alvin Goldman's **reliabilism** — the most influential externalist theory — holds that a belief is justified when it is produced by a cognitive process that reliably produces true beliefs. Perception, memory, and sound inference are reliable under normal conditions; wishful thinking and hasty generalisation are not. Crucially, the believer need not know that their process is reliable. A child who learns to read faces and accurately perceives emotional states is epistemically well-placed even if she could not describe the neural mechanisms enabling her skill.

This makes externalism attractive for thinking about non-human animals, pre-reflective humans, and expert practitioners who know *that* something is the case before they can explain *why*. The experienced doctor who diagnoses correctly on pattern recognition before she can articulate her reasoning seems to have knowledge, not just lucky guessing.

## The clairvoyance problem and the new evil demon

Both positions face a signature counterexample, and examining each is the most direct way to test the theories.

Against externalism, philosophers raise cases like **Norman the clairvoyant**. Norman has a perfectly reliable faculty for knowing the location of the president at any given moment, but he has no idea that he has this faculty, no reason to think he does, and no evidence to back up his spontaneous beliefs about the president's whereabouts. According to reliabilism, Norman's beliefs are justified — they are produced by a reliable process. But intuitively, Norman seems epistemically irresponsible. He has no idea where his conviction comes from. If someone challenged him, he would have nothing to say. Most people feel that Norman does *not* have justified beliefs, which is a point for internalism.

Against internalism, philosophers raise the **new evil demon** case in a different light. Suppose there are two people: one lives in the actual world and forms beliefs through reliable perception; the other is a brain in a vat, deceived at every turn, but forming beliefs through exactly the same *internal* process. According to internalism, both are equally justified because their internal states are identical. But there seems to be a real epistemic difference between them. The first person's beliefs track reality; the second person's do not. Externalists argue that internalism, by ignoring this difference, describes justification in a way that severs it entirely from truth — which should worry anyone who thinks epistemic justification is in the business of getting things right.

## What this means for religious and moral belief

The internalist/externalist divide has direct consequences for how we evaluate the justification of religious belief. A believer who has had a vivid religious experience, who has reflected carefully on it, and who holds it as a datum they cannot explain away — is that belief justified? The internalist asks: is the experience a good reason, given everything else you know? The externalist asks: was the experience produced by a reliable process, one that tracks something real?

The externalist framing opens space for what Alvin Plantinga called **proper function** accounts. A belief is warranted, on his view, if it is produced by cognitive faculties functioning properly in an environment they were designed for, aimed at truth. Plantinga argues that if theism is true and humans were designed to perceive God, then theistic belief can be warranted even without argument — it functions like perception. Critics note that this is a significant conditional: the warrant depends on the disputed theological premise being true. It is not an independent argument for theism; it is a defence of theism's rationality *given* theism, which is a more modest claim.

For moral beliefs, the split is equally consequential. Moral intuitions feel compelling from the inside — they seem like perception of something real. The internalist must ask whether intuitions can serve as genuine evidence. The externalist must ask whether the processes generating moral intuitions — evolutionary pressures, social conditioning, emotional responses — are reliable guides to moral truth. If moral intuitions were shaped by evolutionary fitness rather than moral reality, their reliability is questionable, which threatens the externalist vindication of moral knowledge just as much as it threatens naive intuitionism.

## Why the debate resists clean resolution

The reason this dispute has continued for decades without decisive resolution is that both sides are tracking something real. Justification plays at least two distinct roles in our thinking. It is a **normative concept** — it evaluates whether you are doing well as a believer, whether your epistemic conduct is defensible. And it is a **truth-connected concept** — it marks off beliefs that are not merely held but that are likely to be true, that stand in the right relation to the facts.

Internalism handles the normative role better. Externalism handles the truth-connection better. Hybrid views — there are several — try to honour both, usually by distinguishing between different components of knowledge or by distinguishing justification from warrant or entitlement.

The practical upshot is this: when evaluating any system of belief, it is worth asking both questions separately. Is the believer responding rationally to the evidence available to them? And is the process by which they came to believe this one that actually tends to produce true beliefs? A person can score well on one question and poorly on the other. The evangelical who has studied theology carefully and follows the internal logic of their tradition may satisfy the internalist standard while failing the externalist one, if the original source of the tradition's authority is unreliable. Conversely, the scientist who correctly reads instrument output without being able to explain the underlying physics satisfies the externalist standard while perhaps failing a strict internalist audit.

Good epistemic practice probably requires attending to both. Asking for reasons — the internalist's demand — keeps inquiry honest and communicable. Asking whether the process is reliable — the externalist's demand — keeps inquiry anchored to truth rather than internal coherence. Neither question alone is sufficient. The debate between internalism and externalism is best understood not as a contest one side will eventually win, but as a reminder that justification is doing more than one job at once.
