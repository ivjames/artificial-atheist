The world seems full of things that are not there. There is no dragon in this room, no gold mountain in Nevada, no married bachelor anywhere. These are true statements, and their truth demands explanation — which turns out to be surprisingly difficult to provide.

## What a negative fact is supposed to be

A **positive fact** records the presence of something: this table is brown, water contains oxygen, Caesar crossed the Rubicon. A **negative fact** records an absence: there are no unicorns, the butler did not commit the murder, the patient shows no fever. Both kinds of statement can be true or false. The puzzle is ontological: what makes negative statements true?

The philosopher Bertrand Russell took the problem seriously enough to argue, in his 1918 lectures on logical atomism, that the world must contain negative facts as genuine constituents of reality — facts that are not just the absence of positive facts but are themselves something. His audience reportedly booed. The reaction is understandable. It seems strange to say that the non-existence of a dragon is itself a thing in the world, as real as the chair it is not sitting on. But the alternative — saying that negative truths are made true by nothing at all — seems equally strange, since it detaches truth from reality in a way most people find unacceptable.

This is not a merely academic puzzle. The status of negative facts bears directly on how we reason about evidence, absence, and inference. Anyone who has argued that the lack of evidence for a god constitutes evidence against one has implicitly taken a position on this question.

## The truthmaker problem

Contemporary metaphysics frames the issue through **truthmaker theory**: for every true proposition, there must be something in the world — a truthmaker — whose existence is what makes it true. Positive propositions have obvious candidates. "The cat is on the mat" is made true by the cat, the mat, and the relation between them. But what makes "there is no cat on the mat" true? There is, by stipulation, nothing there to do the job.

Several responses have been tried. One, associated with David Armstrong, posits a **totality fact** — a higher-order fact that records that the universe contains all and only the things it contains. On this view, the absence of a cat on the mat is made true by the total inventory of the world, which simply does not include that cat-on-mat combination. The negative truth falls out as a consequence without requiring negative facts as separate items.

A rival view, **truthmaker nihilism**, bites the bullet and says negative truths simply have no truthmakers. Truth, on this account, does not require a corresponding positive reality for every true sentence. Some truths are true because of what is not the case, full stop. Critics find this unsatisfying: it seems to say that some truths float free of reality entirely, which makes the concept of truth do less explanatory work than we want it to.

A third option is **maximalism about negative entities**: accept that absences, lacks, and holes are genuine features of the world. The hole in the donut is not the donut; it is a real spatial region with its own properties. The shadow on the wall is not the object casting it but has a causal role — it keeps the patch of ground cool, it signals the time on a sundial. On this view, absences can be causes and can serve as truthmakers for negative claims. The world has more in it than objects and properties; it also contains structured lacks.

## Causation by absence

The causal role of absences is particularly contested. Common sense happily accepts absence causation: the plant died because you forgot to water it, the bridge collapsed because the inspector failed to notice the crack, the child drowned because no one intervened. In each case, what caused the outcome seems to be something that did not happen.

Standard counterfactual accounts of causation — where C causes E if, had C not occurred, E would not have occurred — handle this with apparent ease: had you watered the plant, it would not have died. The absence of watering counterfactually explains the death. But this generates an embarrassment of causes. Had any of a trillion other possible interventions taken place, the plant would also have survived. Does every possible but unrealised action that would have saved the plant count as a cause of its death? The gardener next door also did not water it. Was she equally responsible?

Phil Dowe and others have tried to restrict causation to the transmission of conserved physical quantities — energy, momentum — which an absence cannot transmit because there is nothing there to transmit anything. On this view, absence causation is not genuine causation but a kind of shorthand we use because certain absences are salient to us. The real causes are always positive events; the language of "caused by absence" reflects human interest, not metaphysical structure.

This deflationary line is clean but has costs. It seems to misrepresent how responsibility, medicine, and law actually work. Negligence is a legal category precisely because failing to act can cause harm. If the doctor does not administer the antidote, the patient dies of something the doctor could have prevented. Courts, rightly, hold that responsible. Dissolving absence causation into "mere salience" struggles to account for why some absences are salient in a way that tracks genuine moral and causal structure.

## Absence of evidence and evidence of absence

The philosophical status of negative facts has direct implications for one of the most common argumentative moves in discussions about god, the paranormal, and pseudoscience: the inference from absence of evidence to evidence of absence.

A widespread but confused slogan holds that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. The slogan is false as a general principle, though it identifies a real error when applied carelessly. The correct analysis is Bayesian: absence of evidence is evidence of absence **when and only when** we should have expected to find the evidence had the thing existed. If the thing would have left traces, and we searched carefully and found none, the probability of the thing's existence decreases. How much it decreases depends on the thoroughness of the search and the strength of the expectation.

The failure mode the slogan is really warning against is the inference from "we have not searched" to "it is not there." No one has explored every cubic metre of the deep ocean; the absence of a particular species in explored waters is weak evidence against its existence given how little we have looked. By contrast, if a proposed entity would necessarily produce detectable effects — if the prayers of millions should sometimes cause spontaneous tumour regression at rates exceeding the base rate, if divine intervention in history should leave systematic documentary traces — and we look carefully and find nothing, the absence is informative. It shifts the probability.

This matters beyond theology. Failure to detect a drug's effect in a properly powered trial is evidence that the effect does not exist, not merely evidence that we did not look. Null results are results. The confusion between "not found" and "not there" corrupts reasoning in medicine, criminal justice, and public policy, not just religion.

## Holes, shadows, and the ontology of lacks

The metaphysics of absences becomes vivid when considering objects we routinely quantify and describe: holes, shadows, silences, and gaps. Lewis and Lewis (David Lewis and Stephanie Lewis) wrote a playful but serious 1970 paper treating **holes** as immaterial entities that are distinct from the matter surrounding them. A hole in a sock is not the sock; it is something the sock has. When the sock is mended, the hole ceases to exist. When you move the sock, the hole moves with it. This is ontologically extravagant — a commitment to the existence of immaterial, spatially located entities — but it makes everyday speech about holes literally true rather than requiring a paraphrase that is nearly always more cumbersome.

The alternative is to paraphrase away: instead of saying "there is a hole in the sock," say "the sock is perforated in such-and-such a way." No hole is introduced into one's ontology; the negative feature is attributed as a property of the positive object. This is the strategy of **ontological parsimony**: resist multiplying entities beyond necessity, and show that apparent negative entities can be eliminated in favour of positive properties of existing things.

The debate between maximalism and parsimony about lacks is not decisively settled. The choice turns partly on what one takes the goal of ontology to be — faithful representation of common-sense structure, or the sparest possible inventory that handles the logical data.

## What this means for reasoning under uncertainty

Negative facts are not just metaphysically troubling; they are epistemically important in practice. Recognising that absences can be facts — that the non-existence of something can be a real feature of the world rather than a mere failure to find — disciplines how we handle evidence.

Science is full of productive absences. The failure to find a stellar parallax before the 19th century was genuine evidence that either the stars were inconceivably far away or the Earth was not moving — evidence that bore on heliocentrism. The absence of a detection in early neutrino experiments constrained what neutrinos could be. The non-detection of proton decay continues to constrain grand unified theories. In each case, nothing was found — and that nothing did work.

Sloppy handling of negative evidence cuts in both directions. Overclaiming absence — concluding that a single failed search refutes a hypothesis — is bad reasoning. Dismissing absence — treating every null result as merely "we haven't looked hard enough yet" — is equally bad reasoning, and is the characteristic move of unfalsifiable belief systems. The question to ask is always: given that this entity exists, how probable is it that a search of this kind would find nothing? If the answer is "very improbable," then the null result is strong evidence against existence. If the answer is "quite probable," the null result is weak.

That calculation requires honesty about prior probabilities, search sensitivity, and the predictive content of the hypothesis being tested. It is difficult to do well and easy to rig. Which is precisely why negative facts deserve philosophical attention rather than dismissal: the structure of absence is, in the end, the structure of falsification itself.
