Religious knowledge is distributed unevenly inside religious communities, and the pattern is counterintuitive: the people most likely to know what a tradition actually teaches are often those who arrived by choice rather than by cradle. This is not merely an anecdote — surveys, theological educators, and the converts themselves consistently confirm it.

## What the data on religious literacy show

The Pew Research Center's 2010 U.S. Religious Knowledge Survey found that atheists and agnostics scored highest overall, but within religious groups a more specific pattern emerged: people who had actively chosen their faith — converts, returnees after a period of lapse, people who had shopped denominations — consistently outscored those who had never questioned their membership. Subsequent Pew surveys in 2019 reinforced this: self-described Christians could answer, on average, fewer than half the questions about Christian history and doctrine put to them.

This is not about intelligence. It is about **motivated inquiry**. Someone raised Baptist in a Baptist town has little reason to read the Nicene Creed carefully, to understand what separates Arminianism from Calvinism, or to know why the Council of Chalcedon in 451 CE fractured Christianity in ways still visible today. The tradition is ambient, like air. You breathe it; you do not study it.

## Why conversion demands a different kind of engagement

An adult who converts to Catholicism must pass through the Rite of Christian Initiation for Adults (RCIA), a formal process of instruction lasting months. A person born Catholic receives the sacraments on a schedule determined by age, with catechesis that is often perfunctory and concludes at confirmation — typically in early adolescence, before most people are equipped to evaluate any complex system of thought.

The same asymmetry appears in Islam. Someone who takes the shahada as an adult in a Western country usually does so after reading the Quran in translation, encountering hadith literature, and making deliberate comparisons with their prior worldview. A person born into a Muslim family in a Muslim-majority country may have memorised Quranic passages in Arabic without understanding them semantically, and may hold beliefs about Islamic law that scholars of fiqh would not recognise as mainstream.

Judaism is a partial exception worth examining. The bar and bat mitzvah tradition does require genuine textual study, and adult education has a higher cultural prestige in many Jewish communities than in Christian ones. But even here, the Reform Jew who converts in adulthood often has a more systematic grasp of halakha than the Conservative Jew who never left the movement — precisely because the convert had to ask what the rules were rather than simply absorbing them as background custom.

## The epistemics of inherited belief

There is a structural reason for this knowledge gap that goes beyond laziness or poor religious education. **Inherited belief** functions, psychologically, more like identity than like hypothesis. When you are raised in a tradition, its claims are not experienced as propositions you might evaluate; they are experienced as features of the social world, like knowing which side of the road to drive on. Questioning them feels not like intellectual inquiry but like betrayal.

This is not a defect peculiar to religion. People who grew up in strongly ideological households — Marxist, libertarian, nationalist — show similar patterns: confident attachment with shallow justification. The philosopher Miranda Fricker's work on **epistemic injustice** touches adjacent territory: when a belief system is part of one's identity, the social costs of examining it critically are real, not imaginary.

But religion makes this identity-embedding especially powerful because it connects to metaphysical comfort, community belonging, ritual life, and the existential questions that matter most — death, suffering, moral obligation. The incentives against rigorous internal examination are exceptionally strong.

## What converts bring to theological discourse

This creates an odd dynamic inside religious institutions. Converts frequently become disproportionately prominent in theology, apologetics, and religious writing — not because converts are smarter, but because they are the people who already demonstrated a willingness to treat religious claims as claims, evaluate them, and act on the evaluation.

G.K. Chesterton, a convert to Catholicism, wrote some of the most widely read Catholic apologetics of the twentieth century. C.S. Lewis, who moved from atheism through theism to Anglican Christianity, became the most-read popular Christian apologist in the English language. Malcolm X's transformation through Nation of Islam and then to Sunni Islam produced some of the most searching Islamic self-examination in American public life. Thomas Merton, a convert, shaped mid-century Catholic monasticism more than almost any cradle Catholic of his generation.

These are not outliers chosen for rhetorical convenience. Ask any seminary faculty which students ask the hardest questions and demonstrate the most thorough preparation, and the answer tends to be adult converts. They arrive having already done the adversarial reading; they know the objections because they used to hold them.

## The counterargument: depth vs. breadth

The strongest objection to this picture is that converts may know more theology while knowing less *religion*. Theology — systematic, propositional, historical — is only one layer of a tradition. Living religion includes the emotional texture of childhood prayers, the embodied knowledge of liturgical rhythm, the unspoken grammar of a community's life, the way grief is handled at a particular kind of funeral.

A convert who can cite the filioque controversy cannot necessarily hold a dying member of the congregation with the ease of someone who was held that way themselves as a child. The **tacit knowledge** of a tradition — what Michael Polanyi called knowledge that we can do without being able to say — is harder to acquire after childhood and is not captured on any religious literacy survey.

This is a genuine asymmetry, not a dismissal. It means the converts' epistemic advantage is domain-specific. They tend to know more about what the tradition claims, why it claims it, what alternatives it rejected, and how it defends those claims under pressure. They tend to know less about how it feels from the inside when you have never known anything else.

## What this means for evaluating religious claims

For anyone — atheist, agnostic, or believer — trying to think clearly about religion, the converts' advantage has a practical implication. If you want to understand what a tradition actually holds, the people who chose it as adults are often better informants than those who were born into it, precisely because they can articulate the reasons rather than simply inhabit the conclusions.

This matters for the quality of religious dialogue. When critics of religion take on a caricature of the tradition — attacking folk beliefs that the tradition's own theologians would disavow — they are, in part, attacking the version held by the uninstructed majority rather than by those who have studied the system carefully. The rejoinder that most believers hold the folk version, not the sophisticated one, is fair but incomplete: if you want to evaluate whether the claim is *true*, you should evaluate the best version, not the most common one.

Conversely, when religious apologists invoke sophisticated theological positions to deflect objections, they should acknowledge the gap between those positions and what the pews actually believe. Both moves — attacking only the folk version, defending only the elite version — are rhetorical rather than honest.

The converts' knowledge advantage is, in the end, a small illustration of a larger principle: belief arrived at through deliberate inquiry, with genuine exposure to alternatives and a real possibility of concluding otherwise, tends to be better understood by the believer than belief that was simply absorbed. That principle applies far beyond religion, but religion is where the stakes of inherited, unexamined belief are highest — and where the asymmetry is consequently most visible.
