Infallibility is one of the most consequential ideas in organised religion, yet it receives far less critical scrutiny than it deserves. It is not simply an abstract doctrinal claim — it is a structural feature that shapes how authority is exercised, how dissent is treated, and how institutions survive or fracture under pressure.

## What infallibility actually claims

Infallibility is not merely the claim that something is very probably correct, or that its author was unusually wise. It is the claim that a source — a text, a person, or a formal pronouncement — cannot be wrong in matters falling within its defined scope. Error is not just unlikely; it is, by definition, impossible.

The most precisely articulated version is Roman Catholicism's dogma of papal infallibility, defined at the First Vatican Council in 1870. When the pope speaks *ex cathedra* — from the chair of Peter, on matters of faith and morals, with the intention of binding the whole church — the pronouncement is held to be preserved from error by divine assistance. The scope is narrow on paper: very few statements have ever been formally issued under this definition. But the doctrine does more institutional work than its narrow application suggests, because it establishes the principle that a human office can in certain conditions transcend the possibility of error.

Protestant traditions typically relocated infallibility from a person to a text. **Biblical inerrancy** — the doctrine that scripture contains no errors in whatever it affirms — performs the same structural function. It removes the text from ordinary epistemic evaluation. You can investigate what scripture means; you cannot legitimately conclude that it is simply wrong. Islam's relationship with the Quran follows a comparable logic, treating the text as the direct word of God, uncreated and incorruptible. In each case, the mechanism differs, but the epistemic effect is the same: a source is placed beyond the reach of falsification.

## The structural problem, not the theological one

The philosophical objection to infallibility is well known: no finite process — whether a church council, a human being, or a textual tradition transmitted by human hands — can produce a reliable guarantee of its own correctness. The history of scriptural interpretation alone demonstrates this. Christians have derived from the same inerrant text arguments for and against slavery, for and against the ordination of women, for and against usury. If the text is infallible, the disagreement must lie entirely in interpretation — but interpretation is carried out by fallible humans, which means infallibility is doing very little actual epistemic work.

That is a genuine philosophical problem, but it is not the focus here. The more underappreciated issue is institutional. Once you build an organisation around an infallible source, you create a specific set of pressures on how the organisation handles internal disagreement, external criticism, and the passage of time.

## How infallibility reshapes internal dissent

In any ordinary institution, if an authority makes a bad decision, the standard corrective process is critique followed by revision. The revision carries some cost — it implies the earlier decision was mistaken — but the cost is manageable. Institutions do this routinely.

Infallibility forecloses this path for the categories it covers. If a pronouncement cannot be wrong, then a later body cannot straightforwardly reverse it. It can reinterpret, nuance, or contextualise — and in practice this is exactly what happens — but it cannot say "the earlier pronouncement was incorrect." This asymmetry produces a characteristic pattern. Rather than revision, institutions practice **creative reinterpretation**: previous statements are held to be consistent with new positions, even when the tension is visible to anyone reading both texts plainly.

The Catholic Church's handling of the Index of Forbidden Books is a minor but clear example. Formally abolished in 1966, it was not declared to have been a mistake. The Second Vatican Council's *Dignitatis Humanae* affirmed religious liberty in terms that are difficult to square with earlier papal condemnations of liberalism and the "liberty of perdition," but the church did not frame this as correction. The official position remained that development of doctrine is not contradiction.

This is not hypocrisy, exactly. It is what happens when an institution with an infallibility commitment tries to adapt to changed circumstances honestly. The strain shows. Theologians spend considerable energy on what is sometimes called **doctrinal archaeology** — excavating earlier formulations in search of readings that permit continuity. This is intellectually demanding work, and much of it is sophisticated. But it answers to a constraint that ordinary inquiry does not face: the conclusion is fixed in advance.

## The treatment of dissidents

Because infallibility removes certain questions from legitimate debate, anyone who presses them is not engaging in ordinary scholarly disagreement. They are, by the logic of the system, denying a certainty rather than contesting a probability. This shifts how institutions respond to them.

The history of theological censure is long and varied, but the pattern recurs. Hans Küng, the Swiss Catholic theologian, was stripped of his licence to teach as a Catholic theologian in 1979, principally because he disputed papal infallibility directly. The response was not a published rebuttal followed by continued debate; it was removal from an authorised position. Charles Curran, an American moral theologian, was similarly removed in 1986 after dissenting from the teaching on contraception. In both cases, the institution's logic was coherent on its own terms: if the teaching is infallible, dissent is not a position to be debated but a boundary to be enforced.

Protestant variants produce similar dynamics, even without formal machinery. In communities committed to biblical inerrancy, scholars who reach conclusions that imply scriptural error — on historical chronology, on the authorship of texts, on factual claims about the natural world — face professional and social consequences that go beyond ordinary academic disagreement. The loss of a teaching post at a confessional institution is a real career cost. The social pressure in tightly bounded communities can be more severe still.

None of this is unique to religion: secular institutions also punish unorthodox views. But they typically lack the theological justification for treating disagreement itself as a category error. That justification is precisely what infallibility supplies.

## Infallibility and the passage of time

Perhaps the deepest institutional cost of infallibility is temporal. An ordinary institution can look at its nineteenth-century positions on race, gender, or economics and say: we were wrong, and here is what we now understand better. An institution with infallibility commitments covering those same domains cannot say this without threatening its foundational claim.

The result is a characteristic lag. Positions that the broader moral culture has moved on from are defended — with diminishing conviction, in increasingly nuanced formulations — not because the institution's members privately believe them, but because revision would carry too high a cost. Surveys of Catholic laypeople on contraception, divorce, and remarriage consistently show that majorities in many countries reject the official teaching. The teaching endures not because it commands assent but because changing it would raise the question of what else might change, and what the infallibility claim is actually worth.

This is sometimes called the **doctrine of irreversibility** problem. A claim made infallibly cannot be quietly retired the way a non-infallible policy can. Every candidate for infallible statement therefore carries a long shadow: the institution is committing not just its present self but all future iterations of itself to a position, without any mechanism for revision if circumstances make that position untenable.

## Whether infallibility can be reformed from within

Some theologians have proposed solutions. One is the restriction of infallibility claims so tightly that they cover almost nothing controversial — in effect, making infallibility a rarely-exercised formality rather than a living constraint. Another is the development of robust theories of **doctrinal development**, most famously associated with John Henry Newman, which try to explain how genuine change can occur without contradiction. A third, more radical, is the proposal that infallibility should be reunderstood as a property of the whole community of believers over time rather than of any single office — what some call **sensus fidelium**, the sense of the faithful — which at least distributes the claim across a larger and more self-correcting body.

Each of these moves has philosophical merit. But each also dilutes what infallibility originally claimed to provide: certainty, not consensus; divine guarantee, not historical accumulation. If infallibility is hedged to the point where it covers only what the community already broadly accepts, it has done a very different kind of work than what Vatican I had in mind.

## The deeper epistemic point

What infallibility reveals, when examined institutionally rather than just philosophically, is how difficult it is to build a large, long-lived organisation around a claim that is immune to revision. The claim provides enormous short-term benefits — it settles disputes, concentrates authority, insulates doctrine from outside pressure — but it accumulates long-term costs that compound over centuries.

Those costs show up not in abstract theology but in real outcomes: how dissenters are treated, how abuse scandals are managed when institutional credibility is bound up with divine guarantee, how slowly institutions adapt to changed moral understanding, and how much energy is spent in interpretive gymnastics that ordinary scholarly communities would spend on straight revision.

This does not settle the question of whether any infallibility claim is true. It does mean that anyone evaluating the claim needs to look not only at the theological arguments but at the institutional track record of building on that foundation. The evidence there is considerable, and it points in one direction.
