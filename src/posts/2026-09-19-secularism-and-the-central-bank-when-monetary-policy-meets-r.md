Central banks set interest rates. That sentence is so routine it barely registers — until you recall that for roughly a quarter of the world's population, charging or receiving interest is not merely unwise but prohibited by religious law. The collision between secular monetary institutions and religiously grounded financial practice is one of the least-examined fault lines in the broader secularism debate, and it rewards careful attention.

## What Islamic finance actually prohibits and why

The Arabic term is **riba**, usually translated as "usury" or "interest," though the scope of the prohibition is contested among scholars. The core objection is not simply to excessive rates — it is to any guaranteed return on a loan as such. Money, in classical Islamic jurisprudence, is a medium of exchange, not a commodity that can itself be rented out. Profit is legitimate when it accompanies genuine risk-sharing; a fixed return that flows to the lender regardless of the borrower's outcome is not.

This is a principled economic position, not an arbitrary taboo. Several secular critics of financialised capitalism have made structurally similar arguments without any religious framing. The difference is that in Islamic law, the prohibition has institutional weight: it is embedded in fiqh (jurisprudence), enforced by Sharia boards, and increasingly codified in the domestic law of states that have adopted some version of Islamic finance regulation.

The instruments that have evolved to work around riba — murabaha (cost-plus financing), ijara (lease-to-own), sukuk (asset-backed certificates) — are genuine financial innovations, not mere cosmetic rebranding. A murabaha contract, for instance, involves the bank actually purchasing an asset and reselling it to the customer at a marked-up price, with deferred payment. The economic outcome may resemble a loan with interest, but the legal structure is different, and for observant Muslims that difference is morally decisive.

## The secular state's indifference problem

A secular monetary authority — the Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, the Bank of England — operates on the assumption that money is neutral with respect to religion. When the ECB raises its benchmark rate to combat inflation, it does not ask whether some citizens are religiously barred from passing that cost on to borrowers. When a government issues bonds that pay a coupon, it does not design the instrument with observant Muslim investors in mind.

This is not hostility; it is indifference. And that indifference has a cost. Citizens whose religious convictions prevent them from holding interest-bearing savings accounts, taking conventional mortgages, or investing in standard bond funds are, in effect, excluded from large portions of the financial system that secular states have constructed and implicitly normalised.

The secular response to this exclusion tends to take one of two forms. The first is accommodationist: allow Islamic finance products to operate under adapted regulatory frameworks, as the United Kingdom did when it issued its first sovereign sukuk in 2014, becoming the first non-Muslim-majority country to do so. The second is strict neutralist: the state designs its financial system for the general case; individuals with special requirements may use the private market to find compliant products. Both positions are defensible under secular principles, and the tension between them maps onto a wider debate about whether secularism demands neutrality-as-indifference or neutrality-as-equal-facilitation.

## When accommodation becomes endorsement

The accommodationist path raises its own difficulties. When a secular state issues a sukuk, it must work with Sharia advisory boards — private bodies of religious scholars who certify whether a financial instrument meets Islamic law. The state is not endorsing Islam as true, but it is formally incorporating religious legal categories into its own regulatory and financial practice. That is a meaningful step.

Compare it to a cleaner case: a secular state that allows kosher food labelling does not certify the food itself, and no religious authority sits inside the regulatory agency. The Islamic finance parallel would require exactly that kind of institutional entanglement, because the compliance judgment is technical, ongoing, and requires scholarly interpretation of religious texts. Different schools of Islamic jurisprudence disagree about which instruments are permissible — the Accounting and Auditing Organisation for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI) in Bahrain has standards that conflict at points with those applied by Malaysian regulators. A secular state that endorses one standard over another is, however indirectly, taking sides in an intra-religious dispute.

This is not a decisive argument against accommodation. It is a reason to be precise about what the state is doing and why. Issuing a sukuk to broaden access to sovereign debt markets is a policy choice that can be justified in secular terms — increasing financial inclusion, deepening capital markets — without the state claiming any view on the truth of Islamic jurisprudence. The religious compliance certification can be treated as a private-law matter, the way halal certification is handled by private bodies rather than government agencies. But the analogy only stretches so far, because sovereign debt is a uniquely public instrument.

## The harder case: central bank operations and systemic indifference

Sovereign sukuk are a visible and deliberate policy. More challenging is the question of central bank operations, which are not designed with any religion in mind and yet structure the financial environment in which everyone must live.

When interest rates rise sharply — as they did across the Western world in 2022 and 2023 — the cost falls differently on different people. For a Muslim family that had taken out an Islamic mortgage through a specialist lender, the pass-through of rate rises may be slower or different in structure than for a conventional mortgage holder. But Islamic mortgage products are typically more expensive and less widely available, meaning that observant Muslims who live in secular states often pay a premium for access to compliant products — a premium that is, in effect, a tax on religious adherence imposed not by intent but by the structure of a secular financial system.

A strict secularist might say this is not the state's problem: the state does not prohibit Islamic finance, and the premium reflects market thinness, not discrimination. A more expansive reading of equal treatment would note that the state has actively constructed the interest-rate-based system, benefits from it through bond issuance, and implicitly advantages those for whom it poses no religious obstacle. Neither reading is obviously wrong. What is clear is that the question deserves more serious treatment than it usually receives in secular political theory.

## Secular states that have gone furthest

Malaysia offers the most developed example of a plural regulatory approach. Its dual banking system runs a conventional interest-based sector alongside a fully articulated Islamic finance sector, both regulated by Bank Negara Malaysia under a single central bank. The Islamic Financial Services Act 2013 gives Islamic finance its own statutory framework while preserving the conventional system intact. Non-Muslims use Islamic finance products routinely, because some of them — particularly savings and investment accounts — are commercially competitive.

Malaysia is not a fully secular state in the strict sense; Islam is the religion of the federation under its constitution. But the regulatory structure it has built is instructive for secular states, because it demonstrates that a dual system can be administered with technical rigour, that Islamic and conventional products can coexist without one displacing the other, and that non-religious citizens can participate in religiously structured instruments without any doctrinal commitment.

The United Kingdom's approach is lighter-touch. The government has issued sovereign sukuk twice, in 2014 and 2021. The Financial Conduct Authority has worked to ensure that Islamic mortgages receive equivalent treatment under consumer protection law to conventional mortgages, including access to stamp duty relief that applies to the double-transfer of property inherent in some Islamic mortgage structures (which would otherwise trigger double taxation). These are specific, targeted accommodations, each justified on grounds of equal access rather than religious favoritism.

## What secularism actually requires here

The debate about religious finance clarifies something important about secularism as a political value. Secularism does not require that the state be designed as though religion does not exist. It requires that the state not impose religious obligations on citizens and not grant unjustified privilege to religious institutions. These constraints say nothing obvious about whether the state should adapt its financial infrastructure to accommodate the religiously motivated preferences of a significant minority.

The strongest secular argument for accommodation is that financial exclusion is a real harm, that the state's existing system is not religiously neutral in its effects even if it is in its intent, and that targeted adjustments — permitting Islamic finance, adapting tax treatment, issuing compliant sovereign instruments — do not compromise the state's religious neutrality any more than permitting conscientious objection compromises its military authority.

The strongest secular argument against is that religious accommodation in finance creates institutional entanglement with religious legal authorities, privileges one religion's financial preferences over others (Quakers have long objected to certain investments on principled grounds; their objections do not generate bespoke regulatory frameworks), and sets a precedent for religion-specific carve-outs that is difficult to contain.

Both arguments take secularism seriously. The disagreement is not between secularism and religion — it is internal to secular political theory, about what equal treatment of citizens actually requires when their religiously grounded preferences interact with state-constructed systems. That is precisely the kind of question that rewards sustained philosophical attention rather than reflexive answers in either direction.
