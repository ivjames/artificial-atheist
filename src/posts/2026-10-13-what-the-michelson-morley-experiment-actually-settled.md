The Michelson-Morley experiment is routinely listed as one of the most important experiments in the history of physics. What it actually showed, and what conclusions it entitled scientists to draw, is considerably more complicated than the standard textbook story.

## What the experiment was designed to test

In the nineteenth century, physicists had good reason to believe that light, like sound, required a medium through which to propagate. Sound waves are pressure oscillations in air or water. Light was understood to be a wave — Maxwell's equations confirmed it — and waves, in the physical intuition of the era, needed something to wave in. That medium was called the **luminiferous aether**: an invisible, pervasive, presumably rigid-yet-permeable substance filling all of space.

The aether hypothesis was not a fringe idea or an article of faith. It followed from the most successful physical framework of the day. If the aether existed, then the Earth, moving through it at roughly 30 kilometres per second in its orbit around the Sun, should experience an "aether wind." That wind would make light travel at slightly different speeds depending on whether it was moving with or against the flow. Albert Michelson, and later Michelson with Edward Morley, built an interferometer sensitive enough to detect exactly that difference.

The device split a beam of light along two perpendicular arms, reflected both beams back, and recombined them. If the aether wind existed, the two beams would accumulate a measurable phase difference — an interference fringe shift — when the apparatus was rotated. Michelson calculated the expected shift. The instrument, floating on a mercury bath to minimise vibration, was more than sensitive enough to see it.

## What "null result" means and does not mean

The result was, famously, null. No fringe shift appeared at the predicted magnitude. This is often described as a "failed" experiment. That framing is exactly backwards. An experiment that delivers a clear answer — even a surprising one — has succeeded. What failed was the hypothesis being tested.

But here is the subtlety that textbooks tend to elide: the null result did not, on its own, refute the aether hypothesis. It refuted **the specific model** of a stationary aether through which the Earth moves freely. Other aether models remained alive. George FitzGerald proposed in 1889 that physical objects might contract along their direction of motion through the aether by just enough to cancel any detectable fringe shift. Hendrik Lorentz developed the same idea independently and with greater mathematical rigour. The **Lorentz-FitzGerald contraction** was not a desperate ad hoc manoeuvre — it had some theoretical motivation and made specific predictions. It preserved the aether while accommodating the null result.

What this illustrates is a general feature of hypothesis testing in science: data do not arrive with their interpretation attached. Every experiment tests a hypothesis embedded in a network of auxiliary assumptions. When the result conflicts with expectations, the conflict tells you something in that network is wrong, but not which thing. Philosophers of science call this the **Duhem-Quine problem**, and Michelson-Morley is one of its cleanest historical demonstrations.

## Why the experiment mattered anyway

If the null result left multiple interpretations open, why does it deserve its reputation? The answer lies in what happened next, and in the quality of the theoretical pressure the result created.

Over the decade and a half following 1887, several physicists tried to repair aether theory. Stokes proposed that the aether was dragged along by the Earth, which would eliminate the wind locally — but this conflicted with the observed behaviour of stellar aberration. Lorentz's contraction hypothesis, while mathematically coherent, required an increasingly baroque set of assumptions to survive each new test. The aether was acquiring the properties of an entity that could never, even in principle, be directly detected.

This is where the Michelson-Morley result exercised its real leverage — not by refuting a single hypothesis outright, but by placing theorists in a position where every repair to aether theory made it less parsimonious and less predictive. Albert Einstein's 1905 special relativity paper took a different route: it abandoned the aether entirely, not because the experiment demanded it, but because doing so allowed two simple postulates — the constancy of the speed of light and the equivalence of inertial frames — to generate all the results Lorentz had laboured to produce piecemeal. The aether became, in the Machian phrase Einstein admired, a superfluous concept.

This is what the experiment actually settled: not that Einstein was right (he hadn't yet formulated his theory), but that any theory wishing to survive would have to account for the isotropy of light's speed without invoking a detectable medium. The experimental result constrained the solution space. It did not dictate the solution.

## The role of precision and the problem of underdetermination

Michelson and Morley's interferometer was sensitive to fringe shifts as small as 0.01 fringes. The expected shift was around 0.4 fringes. They saw, at most, a drift of about 0.01 fringes — within instrumental noise. Later repetitions of the experiment, with progressively better apparatus, found the same thing. By the 1920s and 1930s, Dayton Miller was claiming to have detected a small positive signal, and for a time this was taken seriously enough to cause Einstein genuine concern.

Miller's result was eventually explained by temperature gradients in his laboratory affecting the apparatus. But the episode illustrates something important: a null result is only as credible as the precision of the instrument and the thoroughness of the systematic error analysis. Scientists did not simply accept Michelson-Morley in 1887 and move on. The experiment was repeated, refined, and contested for decades. The consensus that the aether wind did not exist was built incrementally, not proclaimed on the basis of a single afternoon's data.

This is the ordinary texture of experimental science, and it contrasts sharply with how the experiment appears in popular accounts. The standard story presents a single dramatic moment of discovery. The actual history is a slow accumulation of precision measurements, theoretical competition, and gradually narrowing alternatives — which is exactly how science is supposed to work.

## What it reveals about theory and evidence

The Michelson-Morley experiment is also a useful case study in the relationship between theoretical expectation and experimental design. Michelson was not a theorist; he was a supreme experimentalist. He built the interferometer to answer a specific question posed by the best physics of his time. He did not set out to overthrow anything. The null result surprised him, and he initially thought he had made a mistake.

This is worth dwelling on. The experiment was conceived entirely within the aether framework. It was designed to measure a quantity that the aether framework predicted. In this sense, the very experiment that demolished aether theory was a product of aether theory's internal logic. Scientists who accepted the theoretical framework built the tool that eventually made the framework untenable.

This is a feature, not a paradox. Good physical theories are productive: they generate specific, testable predictions, including predictions about what experiments might detect their own failure. A framework that cannot generate self-critical predictions — that explains whatever result appears — is not doing physics. The aether hypothesis, in its later modified forms, was drifting toward exactly that condition. Special relativity replaced it with a framework that made sharp, novel, quantitative predictions, most of which have since been verified to extraordinary precision.

## What the history should teach us

The Michelson-Morley experiment is sometimes conscripted into a simpler story about the triumph of observation over theory, or about the power of a single experiment to overthrow an established paradigm. Neither story is quite right.

Observation did not triumph over theory — a better theory displaced a worse one, and the experiment helped identify which properties the better theory would need. The paradigm was not overturned overnight by a single result; it was eroded over fifteen years by a combination of experimental precision and theoretical work that showed the aether to be increasingly ad hoc. And the "failure" that textbooks report was, from another angle, a success: an instrument built on the best available science, operated with meticulous care, returning an unambiguous answer to a precisely posed question.

What science requires is not that experiments always confirm hypotheses, but that hypotheses be the kind of thing experiments can, in principle, disconfirm. The luminiferous aether met that standard. The experiment ran. The aether lost. What followed — the physics of the twentieth century, from relativity to quantum field theory — built on the ruins in ways no one in 1887 could have anticipated.

That, more than any simple narrative of discovery, is what makes the Michelson-Morley experiment worth understanding carefully.
