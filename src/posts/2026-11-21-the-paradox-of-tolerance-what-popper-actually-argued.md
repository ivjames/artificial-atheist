Karl Popper introduced the paradox of tolerance in a footnote. That detail matters: a point made in a footnote to *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) has since become one of the most invoked — and most mangled — arguments in popular political philosophy.

## What Popper actually said

The argument is compact enough to quote directly. Popper wrote that unlimited tolerance must lead to the disappearance of tolerance. If we extend unlimited tolerance even to those who are intolerant, if we are not prepared to defend a tolerant society against the onslaught of the intolerant, then the tolerant will be destroyed, and tolerance with them.

Notice what this is and is not. It is not a licence to suppress ideas one finds offensive. Popper was explicit that we should claim the right to suppress intolerance only if the intolerant refuse rational argument and forbid their followers to listen to reason. The trigger is not the content of a view but a specific pattern of behaviour: the deliberate refusal of rational engagement combined with active suppression of others' speech.

Popper also specified the preferred remedy. We should claim the right to prohibit the propagation of intolerant philosophies — but we should do so only *after* attempting to counter them in public debate. Force is a last resort when argument has been foreclosed, not a first response to uncomfortable ideas.

## The structure of the paradox

The underlying logical structure is worth examining on its own terms. Tolerance is a normative commitment: a tolerant society commits itself to permitting a wide range of views and practices. But if that commitment is absolute — if it admits no exceptions — then a movement dedicated to ending tolerance can exploit the commitment to dismantle it. The tolerant society, by its own principles, must permit the movement that will destroy tolerance.

This is a genuine paradox rather than a mere practical problem. It is not simply that tolerant societies face dangerous enemies; the difficulty is that the value of tolerance, taken to its logical extreme, contradicts itself. An absolute principle of toleration contains the seeds of its own negation.

Philosophers distinguish this from a simple contradiction. A contradiction holds two incompatible propositions simultaneously. The paradox of tolerance holds one proposition — be tolerant — whose unconditional application leads, through a causal chain, to its own defeat. The structure resembles other self-undermining norms: a commitment to never coercing anyone could prevent you from stopping a coercer; a commitment to never deceiving anyone might prevent you from protecting someone from a fraudster.

## Where the argument is strongest

The paradox has genuine force in a specific empirical context: when a movement explicitly rejects the norms of open discourse, seeks to prevent its opponents from speaking, and uses legal or institutional mechanisms to entrench its own power. Weimar Germany is the standard historical reference, and not without reason. The Nazi Party used democratic elections, legal procedures, and constitutional freedoms to dismantle the very system that had permitted its rise. A tolerant society that lacked any mechanism for self-defence against this strategy would have been, as Popper suggested, destroyed by its own principles.

The argument is also strong against a naive form of **procedural neutrality** — the view that a liberal state must treat all political positions as equally legitimate participants in democratic deliberation regardless of their content. If some participants in that deliberation are committed to ending the deliberation itself, procedural neutrality is not neutral in its effects. It systematically advantages the anti-democratic actor.

## Where the argument runs into trouble

The paradox is powerful in its intended domain but becomes dangerous when generalised. Three problems stand out.

First, there is a **definitional problem**. Popper's criterion — refusal of rational argument combined with active suppression — is precise but difficult to apply. In practice, the label "intolerant" is cheap. Any sufficiently unpopular or aggressive viewpoint can be described by its opponents as an existential threat to tolerant discourse. If the paradox is invoked whenever a view is deemed sufficiently harmful rather than when it actually forecloses rational engagement, it becomes a tool for ordinary ideological suppression dressed in Popperian vocabulary.

Second, there is a **proportionality problem**. Even granting that some limit on tolerance is justified, the paradox does not specify where that limit falls or what measures are permissible in enforcing it. Suppressing a pamphlet, deplatforming a speaker, prosecuting a political party, and banning a religion all represent different magnitudes of response. The paradox justifies the existence of a limit; it does not calibrate the response.

Third, there is a **recursive problem** that Popper's critics have pressed: who decides which movements are intolerant in the relevant sense? If the authority empowered to suppress intolerance is not itself bound by strong constraints, the paradox can be turned on its head. A government that labels its critics "threats to democratic tolerance" and silences them on those grounds has used Popper's argument to accomplish exactly what Popper was trying to prevent. The cure can replicate the disease.

## What liberal theory has done with it

Popper's argument did not emerge in a vacuum. John Rawls addressed a closely related problem in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), arguing that a just constitutional regime need not tolerate the intolerant when the tolerant sincerely and reasonably believe their own security is in danger. Rawls was careful, however, to tie the permission to suppress intolerance to that security condition — not to the mere offensiveness or falsity of the intolerant view.

John Stuart Mill's harm principle offers a complementary framing. For Mill, the state may restrict speech and action when they cause direct harm to others; the mere expression of a view, however obnoxious, does not meet that threshold. The paradox of tolerance can be read as identifying a class of speech acts — those that actively organise the destruction of open discourse — where Mill's harm condition is plausibly met, without collapsing into a general licence to suppress.

Contemporary legal theorists have tried to institutionalise these distinctions. Many European democracies operate under **militant democracy** (*streitbare Demokratie*) frameworks, which permit the prohibition of parties that seek to undermine constitutional order. Germany's Basic Law contains explicit provisions for this. The framework is a practical attempt to implement Popper's insight while constraining the definitional and proportionality problems — though critics argue it still concentrates too much authority in constitutional courts.

## The epistemological dimension

There is a dimension of this debate that is often missed in political discussions: the paradox of tolerance is partly an epistemological problem, not only a political one.

A tolerant epistemic culture — one committed to hearing out heterodox views, revising beliefs under argument, and refusing to enforce orthodoxy — faces an analogous difficulty. A movement that enters the epistemic community with no genuine interest in evidence or argument, but with the goal of muddying the waters, exploiting good faith, and exhausting its interlocutors, can degrade the epistemic commons without technically violating any rule.

This is the problem that philosophers of science call **manufactured doubt**: the deliberate production of uncertainty about well-established findings not by offering counter-evidence but by mimicking the form of scientific debate while rejecting its norms. Tobacco companies did it. It has been done with climate science. The strategy exploits the epistemic community's commitment to hearing all sides.

The parallel to Popper is direct. A commitment to open inquiry, taken as absolute, can be weaponised by actors who have no interest in open inquiry. Some threshold condition — not "this view is false" or "this view is offensive" but "this actor is not engaging in good faith with evidence and argument" — is needed to protect the epistemic commons, just as it is needed to protect the political one.

The difficulty, again, is that the threshold is easy to abuse. Declaring an opponent to be arguing in bad faith is a convenient way of avoiding engagement with their arguments. Any application of the epistemic version of the paradox requires that the charge of bad faith be itself subject to scrutiny and not merely asserted.

## What the paradox demands of us

Popper's footnote does not resolve into a clean policy recommendation, and that is to its credit. It identifies a genuine structural tension in the commitments of a liberal or open society, insists that the tension cannot be dissolved by simply asserting unlimited tolerance, and sketches the conditions under which restriction is justified — while making clear that restriction is a last resort and that the burden of justification is high.

The demand the paradox makes is not primarily one of action but of precision. It requires that we distinguish between views we find threatening because they are genuinely anti-democratic and views we find threatening because they challenge our preferred conclusions. It requires that we specify what behaviour, not what content, triggers the exception to tolerance. And it requires that the mechanisms we build for defending tolerance are themselves subject to the scrutiny and revision that a tolerant society promises.

That is a harder standard than either "tolerate everything" or "suppress what threatens us." It is probably the right one.
