There is a kind of ignorance that has nothing to do with lacking facts about the world and everything to do with not knowing where you stand in it. Philosophers call this the problem of **self-locating belief**, and it turns out to be far stranger than it first appears.

## What indexicals are and why they matter

An **indexical** is an expression whose reference shifts depending on who utters it, when, or where. "I" refers to whoever is speaking. "Now" refers to the moment of utterance. "Here" refers to the speaker's location. These seem trivially convenient features of language — shortcuts, almost — but they carry a philosophical payload that non-indexical expressions simply cannot carry.

Consider the difference between these two pieces of information. First: "The meeting starts at 14:00 on 12 June." Second: "The meeting starts now." The first is an **objective** claim — it fixes an event to a location in a shared, impersonal time series. The second is an **indexical** claim — it tells you something the first one cannot, namely that the present moment is 14:00 on 12 June. You could know the entire schedule of the universe and still not know whether *now* is the time the meeting starts, because knowing when now is requires a kind of self-location in time that objective facts alone don't supply.

The philosopher John Perry illustrated this memorably. Imagine you are pushing a shopping trolley around a supermarket, following a trail of spilled sugar, trying to find the person making the mess. You know every objective fact about the store, including that aisle three contains a shopper whose bag is torn. And then you realise: that shopper is you. The new piece of information — *I* am the one making the mess — produces an immediate change in your behaviour. But no addition of objective, impersonal facts about the world could have generated that change. What you lacked was not a world-fact but a self-locating one.

## The Sleeping Beauty problem as a test case

The most debated arena for self-locating belief is the **Sleeping Beauty problem**, introduced by philosopher Arnold Zuboff and later popularised by Adam Elga. It runs as follows. Beauty is put to sleep. A fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, she is woken once on Monday and the experiment ends. If it lands tails, she is woken on Monday, given a drug that erases her memory of waking, and woken again on Tuesday. Each time she wakes, she has no way of knowing which day it is or what the coin showed. The question: when she wakes, what probability should she assign to heads?

Two defensible answers have emerged, and they genuinely conflict. The **Halfer** position holds that she should say 1/2. She knew before the experiment that the coin was fair; waking up provides no new information about the coin's objective outcome. The **Thirder** position holds that she should say 1/3. There are three possible situations she could be in — heads/Monday, tails/Monday, tails/Tuesday — and by symmetry, each is equally likely given what she knows. On tails, the experiment produces two wakings for every one it produces on heads, so heads is less likely given that she's awake.

Neither answer is obviously wrong. The halfer is right that no new objective information about the coin has arrived. The thirder is right that self-location among possible awakenings is itself informative. What the problem reveals is that the standard probability calculus, built around impersonal propositions, needs supplementation to handle cases where the evidence is essentially: *I exist, and this is an experience I am having.*

## Why standard epistemology under-serves this

Classical epistemology, from Plato through Descartes to the twentieth-century analysis of knowledge as justified true belief, is built around propositions. A proposition is the sort of thing that is true or false independently of who considers it or when. "Water contains hydrogen" is true regardless of the speaker's identity or temporal location. This framework handles most knowledge claims well.

But self-locating beliefs are not like this. The proposition that captures what you know when you know *I am Perry's messy shopper* is not purely world-directed; it essentially involves an indexical element that ties the content to a perspective. Some philosophers, following David Kaplan's work on the logic of indexicals, distinguish between the **character** of an indexical expression (the rule that fixes reference in each context) and its **content** (what it refers to in a particular context). On this view, "I" has a stable character — always refers to the speaker — but its content varies by context.

The trouble is that when we ask what it means to *believe* an indexical content, things get complicated. If you believe "the meeting starts now" and I believe "the meeting starts now," we seem to believe the same thing — but we may be right or wrong at entirely different times. Our beliefs have the same character but different contents. Standard theories of belief, which treat the objects of belief as propositions, must either individuate those propositions very finely (making them context-dependent) or accept that two people can hold beliefs with the same verbal content that are not the same belief.

## Self-location and the problem of de se attitudes

Philosophers use the Latin phrase **de se** — "of oneself" — to describe beliefs that are essentially about the believer. Roderick Chisholm and David Lewis both argued that de se attitudes cannot be reduced to ordinary propositional attitudes without loss.

Lewis's proposal was radical: instead of taking beliefs to be relations between persons and propositions, we should take them to be relations between persons and **properties**. When you believe you are in aisle three, you are self-ascribing the property of being in aisle three. This is not the same as believing the proposition "Lewis is in aisle three" — a third party could believe that proposition while you fail to believe you are in aisle three, and vice versa.

This matters for debates about consciousness, personal identity, and even theology. A being who knew every objective fact about the universe — every event, every particle, every causal chain — would still lack self-locating knowledge unless it also knew *which* entity in that universe it was, and *when* it existed. Omniscience, as traditionally conceived, quietly presupposes that the omniscient being has no problem of self-location. But why should that be obvious? If God is eternal, the question of which moment is *now* for God becomes genuinely puzzling. Some theologians have noticed this and introduced the idea of God's **eternity as timelessness** precisely to sidestep the problem — but timelessness raises its own difficulties for a God who acts in time.

## Practical consequences of ignoring self-location

These puzzles are not merely academic. **Anthropic reasoning** — reasoning about one's own existence as evidence — arises in cosmology when physicists try to assess the probability of certain physical constants. The fact that we observe a universe compatible with life is not surprising *if* we remember that observers necessarily find themselves in universes that permit observers. But this self-locating fact is easy to misuse. Nick Bostrom's **Simulation Argument** and the **Doomsday Argument** both lean heavily on anthropic reasoning, and both are contested precisely because it is unclear how to count oneself among a reference class of possible observers without smuggling in arbitrary assumptions.

In medicine, base-rate reasoning can go wrong when the patient in front of a doctor is not a random draw from the population. The doctor knows the patient is here, in this clinic, with this complaint — all indexical facts that narrow the relevant reference class significantly. Ignoring self-location produces bad probabilistic inference.

More broadly, any time someone argues from "statistically, most people who believe X are wrong" to "therefore you are probably wrong to believe X," they are assuming that you are a random draw from the population of X-believers. But you have access to your specific evidence, your specific reasoning history, your specific context. You are not just *a* believer; you are *this* believer, here, now. That indexical specificity can legitimately shift the probability.

## What this tells us about the limits of pure objectivism

There is a strand of thought — influential in both scientific and philosophical culture — that holds that genuine knowledge must be **view from nowhere** knowledge: impersonal, fully objective, cleansed of the perspective of any particular knower. Thomas Nagel used this phrase to describe the aspiration of objective understanding, while also noting its limits.

The problem of indexical ignorance is one of those limits. Not all ignorance is ignorance of impersonal facts. Some ignorance is irreducibly perspectival: you don't know who you are among the many, when you are in the sequence, where you stand in the map. And no accumulation of impersonal truths can substitute for that locating knowledge.

This does not validate subjectivism or relativism. The indexical facts — it *is* Monday; this *is* aisle three — are still facts. They are true or false. They are not merely matters of opinion. But they are facts that only become available from a point of view, and any epistemology that ignores points of view will find itself unable to explain a whole class of things we know and a whole class of mistakes we make.

The lesson is modest but precise: knowledge is not just a map of the world. It is also a marker of where the map-reader stands.
