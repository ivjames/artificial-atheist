Language policy is rarely discussed in the same breath as secularism, yet the two are deeply entangled. When a state designates an official language — for courts, schools, legislatures, public signs — it is not making a merely administrative choice. It is choosing whose symbolic world gets to occupy the centre of civic life, and in many societies that choice runs directly through religion.

## The sacred residue inside ordinary languages

Languages do not arrive stripped of their histories. Hebrew was, for centuries, primarily a liturgical language; its revival as a modern vernacular for the Israeli state was inseparable from Zionist identity, which was itself ambivalently religious. Arabic is the language of the Quran, and in the constitutional orders of many Arab states it carries explicit religious weight — the same script, the same phonology, the same classical corpus. Sanskrit retains ceremonial prestige in Hindu nationalist politics precisely because it is the language of the Vedas. Even Latin, long dead as a spoken tongue, persists in Catholic canon law and papal pronouncements because sacred authority accrued to it over centuries.

The point is not that any language is inherently religious. It is that **language embeds cultural memory**, and cultural memory in most of the world's societies is heavily religious. When a state selects an official language, it reaches into that memory and elevates one strand of it. Minority communities whose languages are associated with a different religious tradition — or with no particular religious tradition — find themselves at a structural disadvantage that is also, often, a religious disadvantage.

## The Irish case and the weight of symbolic establishment

Ireland offers an instructive example that is easy to miss. Irish (Gaelic) is the first official language of the Republic under Article 8 of the constitution, ahead of English, the language almost everyone actually uses. The reasons for this are historical and nationalist, but they are also bound up with a Catholic identity politics in which the Irish language was positioned as the authentic tongue of a pre-colonial, Catholic civilisation against the Protestant English coloniser. The revival movement of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — the Gaelic League, the literary renaissance — was not uniformly Catholic, but its emotional grammar was inseparable from that identification.

The result is a symbolic architecture in which the state's primary official language is tightly connected to a particular religious-cultural identity, even though the state is formally secular. Native speakers of that language enjoy legal and administrative advantages. Those advantages are formally neutral, but their historical roots are not. A secular audit of this arrangement asks: does the official status of Irish serve a neutral civic purpose, or does it preserve a religious-cultural primacy that a genuinely neutral state should not be in the business of preserving?

## When script itself is religious battleground

In some contexts, the stakes are even more concrete because the dispute is about **writing systems**, not just spoken languages. The most dramatic modern example is the former Yugoslavia. Serbian and Croatian are, by most linguistic measures, variants of the same language — mutually intelligible, grammatically almost identical. Yet Serbian is associated with the Cyrillic alphabet (and Eastern Orthodox Christianity), while Croatian is associated with the Latin alphabet (and Roman Catholicism). After the dissolution of Yugoslavia, language and script policy became instruments of religious-national differentiation. To insist on one script in official documents was, in effect, to favour one confessional community over another.

Bosnia-Herzegovina navigated this by recognising Bosnian, Croatian, and Serbian as co-official languages under the Dayton framework — a pragmatic solution that acknowledged the religious dimension of the conflict even while never naming it as such. The secular question the Dayton arrangement implicitly answered is: can a state be neutral between confessional communities without being neutral between languages? The answer it arrived at was no.

## The neutrality trap: no language is a clean slate

A common response to all this is to reach for a colonial or global language — English, French — as a supposedly neutral medium that carries no local religious freight. France is the paradigm case. French republicanism has long insisted that the French language, mandatory and universal, is the vehicle of secular citizenship. The *laïcité* tradition holds that the republic has no truck with religious identity, and that French — the language of the Enlightenment, of Voltaire, of the revolutionary Declaration — is the unmarked medium of the universal citizen.

But this is its own kind of myth. French carries its own religious history — it is, among other things, the language of the Gallican church, of Pascal, of countless devotional texts. More importantly, insisting that Breton, Alsatian, Occitan, or Arabic speakers abandon their languages to participate fully in civic life is not neutrality; it is assimilation dressed as neutrality. The communities most affected by French language uniformity — North African immigrants whose Arabic is often the language of their religious practice, Alsatians whose German dialect connects them to a Protestant or Catholic heritage distinct from Parisian Catholicism — experience the language requirement not as an invitation to join a neutral space but as a demand to exit their own cultural and sometimes religious formation.

Secularism that genuinely takes neutrality seriously cannot pretend that one language is the view from nowhere. Every official language is a view from somewhere.

## Linguistic rights as a secular demand

If the problem is that official language policy inevitably privileges some religious-cultural communities over others, one response is to push for robust **linguistic rights** as a component of secular governance. This is the approach taken, imperfectly, in places like Canada, Switzerland, and South Africa.

Canada's Official Languages Act (1969) and the constitutional recognition of both English and French acknowledged that two major linguistic communities — themselves entangled with Protestant and Catholic histories — could not be treated as subordinate one to the other without the state effectively taking sides in a confessional dispute. The settlement is not perfect: Indigenous languages, each with their own spiritual and ceremonial dimensions, remain marginalised in ways the Act did not address. But the underlying logic — that a neutral state cannot have a linguistic monoculture when that monoculture carries religious-cultural baggage — is a recognisably secular one.

South Africa went further after apartheid, recognising eleven official languages in its 1996 constitution. The motivations were primarily about racial and ethnic justice, but the religious dimension is real: Zulu, Xhosa, Sotho, and others are also the languages of oral spiritual traditions, of African Independent Churches, of modes of moral reasoning that exist in no other tongue. Elevating them to official status was, among other things, an act of secularism — a refusal to allow a colonial language to monopolise the civic sphere.

## What a genuinely secular language policy would require

Secularism does not dictate a specific answer to the question of official languages. A state might reasonably have one, two, or several; it might manage language through rights regimes rather than official status; it might invest in translation infrastructure rather than choosing at all. What secularism does require is that the choice be made on grounds that can be defended to all citizens, including those whose religious identity is bound up with a language that is not being elevated.

That standard rules out three common but defective moves. First, it rules out the **ancestral authenticity argument** — the claim that one language deserves official status because it is the tongue of the "original" or "true" inhabitants, when that claim is doing covert religious work (as it often is in Hindu nationalist arguments for Sanskrit or in some Zionist arguments about Hebrew). Second, it rules out **false neutrality** — the pretence that a dominant colonial or majority language is simply the obvious practical choice, when it is also the language of a historically dominant religious-cultural group. Third, it rules out **ignoring the question entirely** — treating language policy as a purely technical matter of administrative efficiency when its religious-cultural effects are significant and predictable.

What remains is harder and more honest: a deliberate reckoning with what a given language carries, who is advantaged and disadvantaged by its elevation, and what compensating rights or accommodations are owed to those who are not at home in it. That reckoning is not anti-religious. It is not a demand that languages be stripped of their histories, which is impossible. It is a demand that the state not use those histories — especially their religious dimensions — as reasons to sort citizens into the deserving and the marginal.

Language is one of the places where secularism stops being an abstract principle about church-state separation and becomes a live question about whose voice the republic is actually willing to hear.
